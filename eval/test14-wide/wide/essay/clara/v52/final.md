**Angle**: Social media is neither good nor bad for democracy in itself; it acts as a stress test, and the result depends on whether a country has fast, trusted and protected referees (a free press, fact-checkers, election courts) and whether its own leaders use the platforms to attack those referees.
**Built from**: 1d (depends on the democracy, say why), 2d (less worn cases: Taiwan, the Philippines, South Korea, Brazil), 3b (school level, structure over citations), 4a (good → bad → verdict).
**Strongest objection**: it can look circular (strong democracies cope because they're strong) and it lets platform design off the hook, since the same outrage-rewarding feeds harmed the United States, which has strong institutions. **Test**: before drafting, take one country not in the outline (the US or India) and check whether the three variables in Section IV predict its outcome. If they don't, add "leaders turning platforms on the referees" as the deciding factor, which is how Section V handles it.
**Other direction**: argue that it's bad everywhere because of the ad-driven feed, and that stronger institutions only delay the damage — the more common line, easier to source, harder to make your own.

# Thesis

Social media has been good for some democracies and bad for others, and the difference is not the technology but what it lands on. Where a democracy has independent courts, election authorities and a press that can correct lies faster than they spread, social media has widened participation and made power easier to watch. Where those referees are weak, or where leaders use the platforms to attack them, it has flooded politics with falsehood and helped strongmen win. Social media is a stress test for democracy, and it scores each country on what it already had.

# Outline (1,500 words)

## I. Introduction (about 150 words)
- Hook: Taiwan and the Philippines are both young Asian democracies where Facebook became, for most people, the whole internet. Both were hit by waves of political disinformation in the same years. One is now cited as a model for handling it; the other as a warning.
- Reframe the question: asking whether social media is "good or bad" is like asking whether a storm is good or bad. The real question is what decides which it becomes for a given country.
- Thesis (above), with a one-line roadmap: the gains, the harms, then what explains the difference.

## II. The good: what social media gives a democracy (about 325 words)
Three gains, each tied to one case, to show they are real rather than hoped-for.
- **Watching power in real time.** Taiwan's 2014 Sunflower Movement: students occupying the legislature livestreamed the chamber for weeks, so the public saw the trade-deal fight directly rather than through party press releases.
- **Organising without a party machine.** South Korea's candlelight protests of 2016–17: weekly rallies coordinated largely online drew millions over months, and ended in a lawful impeachment of President Park Geun-hye through the Constitutional Court, not in the streets.
- **Citizens checking each other.** Taiwan's Cofacts: a volunteer-built bot on the LINE messaging app where users forward suspicious messages and other citizens fact-check them, so correction travels on the same channel as the rumour.
- Pivot: these gains are not accidents of good luck. In each case the platform plugged into institutions (a legislature, a court, a civic-tech community) that could turn online energy into a lawful result.

## III. The bad: what it does where the referees are weak (about 325 words)
Same technology, opposite pattern.
- **The Philippines, 2016.** Rodrigo Duterte's campaign relied on Facebook, cheap to use because mobile carriers offered it free, and on networks of paid and volunteer accounts that flooded feeds with praise and attacks. Journalists who documented this, chiefly Maria Ressa and Rappler, were sued, charged, and had their licence challenged. The main fact-checkers were being prosecuted while the lies spread.
- **The Philippines, 2022.** Ferdinand Marcos Jr. won the presidency after years of YouTube and TikTok content recasting his father's martial-law era as a golden age, with little trusted counter-narrative reaching the same audiences.
- **India, 2018.** Rumours of child kidnappers forwarded on WhatsApp led to mob killings in several states; police and courts could not correct a message that had already reached millions.
- Pattern: in each case, falsehood moved faster than any trusted institution could answer it, and the people best placed to answer were under attack.

## IV. What explains the difference (about 400 words)
The hinge of the essay: three variables that separate Section II from Section III.
1. **Speed and trust of correction.** Taiwan's government and civil society built a habit of answering rumours within hours, often with humour, and had fact-checking groups the public trusted. The Philippines' leading fact-checker was in court.
2. **Authority of election bodies and courts.** Brazil as the test case: in 2018 and 2022, mass WhatsApp and Telegram messaging spread claims the voting machines were rigged; in January 2023 a crowd stormed the Congress, Supreme Court and presidential palace. The result stood because the electoral court and Supreme Court had the authority to act and the public mostly accepted them. The same flood in a country without that authority would have ended differently.
3. **Whether leaders turn the platforms on the referees.** The worst cases are not where lies circulate but where the government itself uses social media to delegitimise courts, election officials and journalists. Duterte against Rappler and Bolsonaro against the electoral court are the pattern; Taiwan's leaders did the opposite.
- Honest cost: Brazil's defence included ordering content removed and, in 2024, suspending X nationwide for weeks. A democracy strong enough to resist the flood may overreach in doing so. The essay should name this trade-off rather than pretend the "good" cases are clean.

## V. Counterargument (about 150 words)
- Objection: the United States has strong courts, a free press and a long democratic record, yet social media is widely blamed for polarisation and election-denial there. Doesn't that show the harm is in the design, not the country?
- Response: the US case fits variable 3. The damage was worst when a sitting president used the platforms to attack election officials and courts; those institutions held, which is why the result stood. The design of the feed makes the storm stronger everywhere, but strong institutions and leaders who respect them decide whether it topples anything.

## VI. Conclusion (about 150 words)
- Restate the verdict in fresh words: social media doesn't make democracies good or bad; it reveals which ones were already resilient and punishes those that weren't.
- Practical implication: the useful question for any country is not "should we have social media" but "are our referees fast, trusted and protected, and will our leaders leave them alone."
- Close by returning to Taiwan and the Philippines: same platform, same decade, same region — and the difference lay entirely offline.
