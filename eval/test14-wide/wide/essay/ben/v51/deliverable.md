# Thesis

Whether social media has been good or bad for democracy is not a single question with a single answer, because social media does not add to or subtract from democracy directly; it replaces whatever intermediary controlled the supply of political information before it arrived. Where that intermediary was state censorship, as in Sudan, Belarus and Iran, social media has been a clear democratic gain, lowering the cost of organising and exposing what the state wished to hide. Where that intermediary was a functioning set of gatekeepers — a professional press, party vetting, editorial judgment — as in Britain and the United States, social media has reproduced the reach of those institutions without their filter, substituting engagement-ranking for editorial judgment, and has been a net loss. The right question is therefore not whether social media is good for democracy but what it is replacing.

# Outline (~1,500 words)

## I. Introduction (~175 words)
- Open with two cases a decade apart that invert the usual pairing. In August 2020, after the Belarusian state cut mobile internet during the post-election protests, the Telegram channel NEXTA, run from Warsaw, kept hundreds of thousands of protesters coordinated for weeks. In July 2024, a false name for the Southport attacker, spread on X within hours, put rioters on the streets of a country with a public broadcaster, a regulated press and no shortage of reliable information.
- Same feed architecture, opposite democratic effects. The difference is not the technology but the institutional baseline it replaced.
- State the thesis. Signpost: the mechanism (II), the two cases it predicts (III–IV), why averaging them misleads (V), the strongest objection (VI).

## II. The mechanism: social media as substitution (~225 words)
- Every political information system has an intermediary that decides what reaches the public. Social media's distinctive feature is that it replaces that intermediary with a ranking system optimised for engagement, available to anyone with a phone.
- Two baselines define the outcome. Against a censoring state, the engagement feed is a vast improvement: imperfect information beats none. Against a working press and party system, it is a downgrade: the old intermediary filtered for accuracy and relevance, and the new one filters for reaction.
- Support: Lorenz-Spreen, Oswald, Lewandowsky and Hertwig's 2023 systematic review in *Nature Human Behaviour* of roughly 500 studies found that digital media's associations with political participation and information access were largely positive, while associations with trust, polarisation and populism were largely negative, and that the positive effects were concentrated in emerging democracies and the negative ones in established ones. The conditional thesis is the pattern in the evidence, not an exception to it.

## III. Where it displaces censorship (~325 words)
- **Sudan, 2018–19.** The Sudanese Professionals Association organised the protests that ended Omar al-Bashir's thirty-year rule largely through Facebook and WhatsApp. The transitional military council's response on 3 June 2019 — a near-total internet shutdown lasting over a month — is the clearest available evidence of the platforms' value: a state does not cut what does not threaten it.
- **Belarus, 2020.** Develop the NEXTA case from the introduction: a state-controlled media monopoly meant the platforms were not competing with journalism but with its absence.
- **Iran, 2022.** The Mahsa Amini protests spread through Instagram and WhatsApp, both of which the state then blocked; the images that reached the outside world did so through the platforms.
- Draw on Zeynep Tufekci's *Twitter and Tear Gas* (2017) for the limit: networked movements assemble fast but struggle to sustain pressure and negotiate, which is why Sudan's revolution was followed by a coup in 2021. Concede this fully. The claim is comparative, not absolute: measured against the baseline of state control, the gain is real even where the movement later failed. Larry Diamond's "Liberation Technology" (*Journal of Democracy*, 2010) was right about this half of the world.

## IV. Where it displaces gatekeepers (~325 words)
- **Britain, 2024.** Return to Southport. The false name and the claim that the attacker was an asylum seeker reached millions on X before any newsroom could correct it; the correction, when it came, did not travel as far. The point is not that British media are flawless but that the country already had an information supply that would have filtered this claim, and social media replaced it with one that rewarded it.
- **United States.** Rather than the 2016 election, use the structural case: the professional incentives of legislators. When the fastest route to national recognition is a viral clip rather than committee work, representatives perform for the feed, and the party gatekeepers who once controlled nominations lose their leverage. This is a substitution of audience for institution.
- Experimental support: Allcott, Braghieri, Eichmeyer and Gentzkow's 2020 *American Economic Review* study paid American users to deactivate Facebook for four weeks before the 2018 midterms and found reduced polarisation on policy issues and reduced news knowledge. Both findings fit the thesis: the platform was supplying information, but a worse kind than the institutions it displaced.
- The conclusion of this section: in established democracies social media did not give citizens a voice they lacked; it gave them a worse intermediary than the one they had.

## V. Why "on balance" misleads (~150 words)
- The two effects occur in different countries and do not net out. A global average that weighs Sudan against Britain produces a verdict that is true nowhere.
- The policy implication differs by baseline. In mature democracies the task is to rebuild the filter — editorial, legal or algorithmic — not to restrict access. In repressive states the task is to keep access open, and the state's shutdowns are the measure of success.
- This also explains why the debate has stalled: both sides are describing real cases and generalising from the wrong half of the world.

## VI. The strongest objection (~200 words)
- **Objection:** the thesis predicts gains in weak or repressive states, but Myanmar (Facebook and the Rohingya, 2017), Sri Lanka (anti-Muslim riots, 2018) and India (WhatsApp lynchings, 2018) were weak states where social media was catastrophic.
- **Response:** these cases refine the thesis rather than refute it. The variable is what social media displaces, not state strength. In Myanmar the military was itself among Facebook's largest political users; the platform did not displace censorship, it extended the state's reach into a population with no prior civic baseline. The conditional holds where the obstacle to democracy is information control; it does not hold where the obstacle is communal violence, and the essay should say so plainly.
- **Second objection:** Boxell, Gentzkow and Shapiro (2017, *PNAS*) found American polarisation rising fastest among the over-65s, who use social media least. Response: the deactivation evidence in Section IV shows a causal effect at the individual level, and polarisation is only one of the outcomes at issue; the gatekeeper loss shows up in information quality and institutional behaviour even where it does not show up in attitudes.

## VII. Conclusion (~100 words)
- Restate in fresh terms: social media is neither a liberation technology nor a corrosive one. It is a replacement intermediary, and its value is the difference between what it offers and what it replaced.
- Close on the Belarus–Southport pairing: in one country the state cut the internet to stop democracy; in the other, democracy's own institutions could not keep pace with a lie. The lesson is not the same in both places, and an essay that gives one answer has read only one of them.
