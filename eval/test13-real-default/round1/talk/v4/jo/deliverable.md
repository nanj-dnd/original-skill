**Angle**: For a room that's mostly not engineers, the useful talk isn't "should we use these" (yes, mostly) but what that changes in what the rest of the company can expect from engineering: some asks get much cheaper, a few get no cheaper, and a short list stays deliberately slow.
**Built from**: 1a (agree with the balanced view), 3b (whole company has to follow it), 4d (no fixed ask, so the close is soft).
**Strongest objection**: engineers in the room may feel they're being explained to rather than talked to. **Test**: read the outline to one engineer and one non-engineer and ask each which section was for them; if the engineer says "none", move section 4 earlier and sharpen it.

## Outline (5 minutes)

**1. Open (0:00–0:40)** — paragraph below. Settle the yes/no quickly, then turn to what it means for everyone else.

**2. What gets cheaper to ask for (0:40–1:50)**
- The work that used to lose on priority because it was too small: internal tools, one-off reports, a quick fix in an old corner of the product, a rough prototype to see if an idea holds up, a plain-language answer to "how does X currently work".
- Why: this is work where the cost was mostly typing and reading, and that cost has fallen a lot.
- Practical change for the room: it's now reasonable to ask for a rough version before a plan. "Can we see something by Thursday" is a fair question more often than it was.

**3. What looks like it should get faster and doesn't (1:50–3:10)**
- The part of a feature that was never typing: agreeing what it should do, fitting it into what already exists, finding what it breaks for real customers, and checking it.
- The assistant can produce a plausible version in an hour; getting it to the point where we'd put it in front of customers still takes the same thinking, and sometimes more, because someone has to read and understand code they didn't write.
- For non-engineers: this is why "but the AI could write that in an afternoon" and "the estimate is a week" can both be true. Offer to show the gap on a real example if anyone wants one.

**4. Where we stay slow on purpose (3:10–4:10)**
- Not a ban. A short list: anything touching customer data, billing, access and permissions, and anything that fails quietly rather than loudly.
- The test: a wrong answer there costs more than the time saved, so a person who can explain the change is the gate, however the first draft was produced.
- Framed as a promise to the company, not a rule for engineers.

**5. Close (4:10–5:00)** — pick one:
- Soft: "When you see us using these tools for most of the work, that's the plan. When you see us slow down on something, ask why; there's usually a reason, and sometimes you'll change our mind."
- Invitation: "If there's something you've wanted built for months that felt too small to ask for, this is the quarter to ask."

## Opening paragraph

> If you're not in engineering, you've probably seen the claims that AI now writes a large share of the code at big companies, and if you've wondered why that doesn't mean every feature here now takes half as long, this is for you. The short version is that AI coding assistants are good, most of us should be using them for most of what we type, and that still isn't the same thing as everything. So I'm not going to spend five minutes on whether engineers should use them; broadly, yes. I'd rather use the time to tell the rest of the company what that actually changes about what you can expect from us: which things really do get faster, which things look like they should and don't, and the short list where we're going to stay slow on purpose.

(About 130 words, roughly 40 seconds spoken.)
