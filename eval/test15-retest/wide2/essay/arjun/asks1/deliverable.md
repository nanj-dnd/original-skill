# Thesis

**Working thesis:**
Asking whether social media is "good" or "bad" for democracy is a category error, because democratic effects follow from specific architectural features rather than from "social media" as a whole. India's WhatsApp lynchings of 2017–18 show that harm arose from one particular combination: encrypted private messaging, frictionless one-tap forwarding, and closed groups of trusted contacts. Together these made rumours both highly persuasive and impossible to hold anyone accountable for. WhatsApp's July 2018 forwarding limit shows that the democratically preferable response is to add friction by design, rather than to restrict content or let the state trace message originators. Design friction slows harmful virality without giving the state a surveillance tool. However, it treats the speed of spread, not the underlying prejudice, so it is necessary but not sufficient.

**Why this works for your prompt:** It's arguable. Someone could reasonably say the problem is prejudice, not design, or that traceability is the better fix. It rejects the binary explicitly. And it makes a normative claim (friction beats censorship and surveillance), not just a descriptive one.

**Shorter version, if you want something punchier:**
The democratic impact of social media depends on its architecture, and India's WhatsApp lynchings show that slowing how messages move is a better democratic remedy than policing what they say or tracing who said them.

# Outline (≈1,500 words)

## I. Introduction (~175 words)

**Hook:** On 1 July 2018 in Rainpada, Dhule district, Maharashtra, a mob killed five men after WhatsApp rumours warned of child kidnappers in the area. This was one of dozens of similar killings across India in 2017–18 (e.g., Karbi Anglong, Assam, June 2018; Bidar, Karnataka, July 2018).

**Move:** Most debate asks whether platforms like this are good or bad for democracy. That framing hides the more useful question: *which design features* produce which democratic effects?

**Thesis:** As above.

**Roadmap:** (1) a framework for treating design as regulation; (2) how WhatsApp's architecture turned rumour into violence; (3) why the forwarding limit beats the alternatives; (4) the strongest objection, that prejudice rather than design is the real cause.

## II. Framework: Architecture as Regulation (~200 words)

**Topic sentence:** To see why the good-or-bad framing fails, we need to treat platform design as a form of governance in its own right.

**Evidence and points:**
- **Lessig, *Code and Other Laws of Cyberspace* (1999)/*Code 2.0* (2006):** behaviour is regulated by four forces: law, norms, markets, and architecture ("code is law"). The forwarding limit is a clear case of regulation through architecture.
- **Distinguish two models of communication:**
  - *Public algorithmic feeds* (Facebook News Feed, X, YouTube): content is visible, so it can be moderated, fact-checked, and studied, but algorithms amplify it.
  - *Private encrypted messaging* (WhatsApp): content is invisible to the platform, so it cannot be moderated. It spreads through personal trust networks, not algorithms.
- **Key claim:** These two models produce different risks and therefore need different remedies. You can't moderate content you can't see, so with WhatsApp the only available lever is design.

## III. How WhatsApp's Architecture Produced the Lynchings (~325 words)

**Topic sentence:** The lynchings were not simply "misinformation online." They resulted from three design features that, combined, made rumours credible, fast, and untraceable.

**Feature 1: Trusted, closed networks.**
- Messages arrive from family, caste, village, and neighbourhood groups, not from strangers. Trust in the sender carries over to the content.
- **Evidence:** Banaji & Bhat, *WhatsApp Vigilantes* (LSE, 2019): users trusted messages from people they knew and from ideologically aligned groups.
- **Optional additional evidence:** the BBC World Service study *Duty, Identity, Credibility* (Chakrabarti et al., 2018).

**Feature 2: Frictionless forwarding that strips context.**
- Before July 2018, a forwarded message looked the same as an original one. There was a quick-forward button beside media, and a message could go to up to 256 chats at once.
- Images and videos, often old or from other countries, travelled without any source information.
- **Evidence:** Garimella & Eckles, "Images and Misinformation in Political Groups: Evidence from WhatsApp in India," *HKS Misinformation Review* (2020): image-based misinformation was widespread in public political groups.

**Feature 3: Encryption makes content invisible.**
- End-to-end encryption means neither WhatsApp nor fact-checkers can see what is spreading until offline violence reveals it.

**Analytical point:** None of these features is harmful on its own. Encryption protects dissidents and journalists, and trusted networks support everyday civic life. *The harm came from their combination.* This is why "good vs. bad" is the wrong question.

## IV. Three Possible Responses, and Why Friction Wins (~375 words)

**Topic sentence:** The Indian case offered three possible responses (content removal, traceability, and design friction), and only friction addresses the harm without damaging democratic values.

**Option A: Content moderation/takedowns.**
- This is impossible on an encrypted platform without breaking encryption.
- The state's fallback has been internet shutdowns. India leads the world in shutdowns (Access Now's annual #KeepItOn reports). Shutdowns are a blunt tool that silence everyone, including people trying to debunk rumours.

**Option B: Traceability.**
- In July 2018, the Ministry of Electronics and IT pressed WhatsApp to act. Later, Rule 4(2) of the IT Rules, 2021 required large messaging platforms to identify the "first originator" of messages.
- WhatsApp challenged this in the Delhi High Court in 2021.
- **Argument:** Traceability gives the state a tool that would also expose government critics. In a context of democratic backsliding, this is a serious cost. It also targets the person who *created* a rumour, when the harm actually comes from thousands of people *forwarding* it.

**Option C: Design friction (WhatsApp's actual response).**
- July 2018: forwarding was limited to five chats in India, the quick-forward button beside media was removed, and a "Forwarded" label was introduced.
- January 2019: the five-chat limit was extended globally.
- April 2020: "highly forwarded" messages were limited to one chat at a time. WhatsApp reported a 70% drop in such messages.
- **Argument:** These changes are *content-neutral*. They apply to all messages equally, they don't require anyone to read messages, and they don't require identifying users. The "Forwarded" label restores some of the context that forwarding strips away.

**Concluding sentence for the section:** Friction is the only option that respects both free expression and privacy, which makes it the most democratically legitimate option.

## V. Counterargument and Rebuttal (~275 words)

**Counterargument 1 (the strongest): the cause is prejudice, not design.**
- Banaji & Bhat argue that misinformation spread because it confirmed existing communal, caste, and anti-outsider prejudices. Victims were often outsiders, migrants, or members of minority groups. On this view, focusing on design lets political actors off the hook.
- **Rebuttal:** Concede the core point. Design doesn't create prejudice. But design determines how *fast* prejudice can mobilise a crowd and how *far* it reaches. A rumour that takes days to spread gives police and local fact-checkers time to respond. This is why the thesis claims "necessary but not sufficient."
- **Supporting detail:** Offline interventions mattered too. Examples include the Supreme Court's guidelines in *Tehseen S. Poonawalla v. Union of India* (17 July 2018) and police awareness campaigns in Telangana's Jogulamba Gadwal district.

**Counterargument 2: friction doesn't actually work.**
- de Freitas Melo et al., "Can WhatsApp Counter Misinformation by Limiting Message Forwarding?" (Complex Networks, 2019): forwarding limits delay the spread of content but don't stop highly viral content from eventually reaching large audiences.
- **Rebuttal:** For lynchings, delay is exactly what matters. Violence happens within a short window of panic, so slowing the spread directly reduces the danger.

**Optional counterargument 3:** It is undemocratic for a private company, rather than an elected parliament, to make these decisions. You could briefly concede this, then argue that design standards could be *mandated* by law, which is a better role for the state than traceability.

## VI. Conclusion (~150 words)

- **Restate the thesis in new language:** The lynchings were a design problem amplifying a social problem.
- **Broaden the point:** The same analysis applies elsewhere. Researchers have studied WhatsApp misinformation during Brazil's 2018 election (Resende et al., 2019). Telegram has similar affordances. Public feeds need different remedies, such as changes to algorithmic ranking.
- **Final line:** Democracies should stop asking whether social media is good or bad, and start asking which design features they are willing to require.

# A Few Cautions

- **Verify every citation** against the original before submitting, especially exact titles, page numbers, and the EPW issue. I'm confident these works exist, but check the details.
- **Don't overclaim causation.** Lynchings declined after mid-2018, but there's no clean evidence that the forwarding limit alone caused the decline, because court guidelines and police campaigns happened at the same time. Frame the limit as reducing *velocity*, which is measurable, rather than as having *stopped the lynchings*. Making this concession openly will strengthen your argument.
- **Use a specific death tally.** Pick one count (e.g., from IndiaSpend's database or Reuters' reporting) and cite it, rather than using a vague number.
