## Thesis

The claim that social media has broken democracy outruns its evidence. Polarization in the United States began rising decades before the platforms existed, and it has diverged sharply across countries that use the same apps. In the largest experiments that changed what people see, attitudes barely moved. Social media's measurable harms are real but concentrated among small, already committed groups. Treating it as the main cause pulls attention away from slower-moving drivers: party elites, partisan cable news and electoral institutions.

## Outline (about 1,500 words)

**I. Introduction (about 175 words)**
- Open with the puzzle. Germany and the United States run on the same Facebook, YouTube and X, yet their trends in partisan hostility since the 1980s point in opposite directions (Boxell, Gentzkow & Shapiro, *Review of Economics and Statistics*, 2024).
- If the platforms were the main engine, outcomes should converge. They don't.
- State the thesis. Give a roadmap: three tests of the alarm, then where the alarm is right.

**II. The alarm at full strength (about 200 words)**
- Lay out the case fairly before testing it. It runs through engagement algorithms, echo chambers, viral falsehoods and the collapse of a shared factual base.
- Name a serious proponent rather than a straw man, such as Haidt's work and the collaborative review he maintains with Bail.
- Turn this into testable predictions. If the alarm is right, polarization should track platform adoption across time and place, fall hardest on heavy users, and respond when feeds change.

**III. Test 1: timing and place (about 250 words)**
- Congressional polarization starts climbing in the 1970s (McCarty, Poole & Rosenthal's DW-NOMINATE data), long before 2004 to 2010.
- Cross-country data (Boxell et al. 2024): affective polarization rose in the US but fell in several democracies with similar internet adoption, including Germany, Sweden, Norway and the UK.
- Point: a common technology can't explain divergent outcomes alone. National institutions and party systems are doing the work.

**IV. Test 2: who polarized, and who saw the falsehoods (about 225 words)**
- Polarization grew fastest among Americans over 75, the group least likely to use social media (Boxell, Gentzkow & Shapiro, *PNAS*, 2017).
- On 2016 Twitter, about 1% of users accounted for 80% of exposure to fake-news sources (Grinberg et al., *Science*, 2019). Sharing such links on Facebook was rare and concentrated among users over 65 (Guess, Nagler & Tucker, *Science Advances*, 2019).
- Point: the harm is real but concentrated in a small, already partisan slice of users. It is not mass brainwashing.

**V. Test 3: the experiments (about 250 words)**
- In the 2023 Meta–academic studies from the 2020 US election (*Science*, *Nature*), switching users to chronological feeds, cutting reshares or reducing like-minded content changed what people saw. None of them measurably shifted polarization or political attitudes over the study period.
- Bail et al. (*PNAS*, 2018) complicate the echo-chamber story further: exposing Twitter users to opposing views made some of them *more* entrenched, not less.
- Point: if the feed were the main lever, pulling it should move something.

**VI. Counterargument: where the alarm is right (about 250 words; the required section)**
- Allcott et al. (*American Economic Review*, 2020) found that deactivating Facebook before the 2018 midterms lowered some polarization measures, mostly on policy views. It also reduced news knowledge, so the effect cuts both ways.
- Lorenz-Spreen et al. (*Nature Human Behaviour*, 2023) reviewed about 500 studies. They found mostly harmful associations with trust and polarization, but beneficial ones for participation and knowledge, with effects varying by country.
- The system-level objection: platforms may reshape elite and media incentives in ways that user-level studies can't capture.
- Rebuttal: concede a contributing role, especially for elite incentives. But the size and spread of the effects still don't support "main cause," and the cross-country divergence in III is hard to square with a platform-driven story.

**VII. Where to look instead, and conclusion (about 150 words)**
- Better-supported drivers include partisan cable news (Martin & Yurukoglu, *AER*, 2017 on Fox News), elite and party polarization, and winner-take-all two-party institutions.
- The stakes: a misdiagnosis invites the wrong cure. Platform fixes alone won't repair problems that started in party systems.
- Close by returning to the Germany–US puzzle.
