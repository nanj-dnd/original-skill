## Thesis

Social media has been good for democracy's voice but bad for its machinery. It lets ordinary people unite and challenge power faster than ever, as the Arab Spring showed, but it does little to build the parties, compromises and trusted institutions that turn protest into lasting self-government. Its effect on any democracy depends on whether those institutions are strong enough to channel the energy it creates.

## Outline (about 1,500 words)

**I. Introduction (~175 words)**
- **Hook:** Egypt, 2011. Protests organized partly through Facebook and Twitter forced Hosni Mubarak out in 18 days. Two years later, the military removed Egypt's first freely elected president, and the country was back under military rule.
- **Question:** If social media could topple a dictator, why couldn't it help build a democracy?
- **Thesis.**
- **Roadmap:** first voice, then machinery, then what decides which one wins.

**II. Framework: what democracy needs (~200 words)**
- **Voice:** people can speak, organize, protest and hold leaders accountable.
- **Machinery:** linkage institutions such as parties, interest groups, elections and the press turn opinions into policy and let opponents compromise.
- **Federalist 10:** Madison argued that a large republic makes it hard for a faction to "discover their own strength" and "act in unison."
- **Key move:** Social media removes the distance that slowed factions down. That helps a majority facing a dictator, and it is risky inside a working democracy.

**III. Good for voice (~325 words)**
- **Arab Spring:** Tunisia and Egypt in 2011. Online networks let people see that others shared their anger, coordinate protests and get images past state media.
- **Why this matters:** It lowered the cost of participation for people with no party or organization behind them.
- **Concede fully:** This is a real democratic gain and should be treated as one.

**IV. Bad for machinery (~400 words)**
- **Egypt after Mubarak:** The movement grew fast but had few leaders, little organization and no shared plan. Better-organized groups, the Muslim Brotherhood and then the military, filled the gap. Sociologist Zeynep Tufekci (*Twitter and Tear Gas*) argues that easy online organizing skips the slow work that builds staying power.
- **India and Myanmar:** Speed without checks. WhatsApp rumors in India were linked to mob killings in 2017–18, and a 2018 UN fact-finding report said Facebook played a "determining role" in spreading hate against the Rohingya. In both cases, information moved faster than any institution could respond.
- **Connect to the frame:** Each case shows voice growing faster than the machinery meant to channel it.

**V. Counterargument and response (~225 words)**
- **Counterargument:** Armies and economics caused these failures, not social media.
- **Response:** Social media didn't cause the failures, but it shaped the kind of movement that formed: fast, broad and loosely organized, which made it easier to defeat.
- **Taiwan:** The vTaiwan project used online tools to help citizens and officials reach agreement on issues like Uber regulation. That shows the tools can strengthen machinery when they are designed and used for it.

**VI. Conclusion (~175 words)**
- **Restate:** Social media amplifies voice, and institutions decide what that voice becomes.
- **Return to the hook:** Egypt had the voice in 2011 but lacked the machinery to keep what it won.
- **Takeaway for an AP Gov reader:** Democracies that keep strong linkage institutions can absorb social media's energy, and weak ones get overwhelmed by it.
