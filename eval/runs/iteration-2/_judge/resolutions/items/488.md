# Count Attempts, Not Outcomes

Every January someone tells you most New Year's resolutions fail. There's usually a number attached, something like 80 percent by February. I've never seen anyone point to the study behind it, but the source isn't my real problem. My problem is what it counts. It watches one attempt break and calls that the outcome.

Count attempts, not outcomes. Most attempts fail. That's true of resolutions and it's true of quitting smoking, which I know because I did it badly three times before I did it once.

I smoked for fifteen years. In 2015, according to the CDC, more than half of American adult smokers tried to quit, and about 7 percent managed it that year. Read that as a verdict and it's grim. But the same report found that most Americans who had ever smoked had quit. Those people didn't skip the failing part. Most people who quit for good needed several tries first. I needed four.

The first three taught me what gets me: the first coffee of the day, driving, my brother. Attempt two lasted five weeks and broke at a wedding, with a drink in my hand.

Every quitting guide tells you to know your triggers. Fine. How? Nobody hands you the list. I didn't know weddings plus drinking would get me until a wedding plus drinking got me. Attempt two didn't fail to produce a quitter. It produced a fact, and that fact went straight into the plan for attempt four.

People call the one that sticks a triumph of willpower. I won't pretend will played no part. I've had to not smoke for six years, and that takes something. But my will didn't change between attempt two and attempt four. What I knew did. By the fourth try I knew about the coffee, the car, my brother, and what happens to me at a wedding once the drinks start. I planned around exactly those.

So when someone says most resolutions fail, ask: most what? If they mean most attempts, yes, and they always will. That number describes how change works, not who is capable of it. If you've relapsed, you aren't outside the count. You're in the middle of it, where most of us who quit for good spent years.

The useful question was never whether this attempt would fail. It's what this one tells you about the next.
