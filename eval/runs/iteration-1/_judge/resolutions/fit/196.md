# Why Most New Year's Resolutions Fail (and It's Not Your Willpower)

Every January, a lot of us make the same quiet promise. This is the year we'll run three mornings a week, read more books, finally learn Spanish. Then by mid-February the running shoes are back by the door, gathering dust.

If that sounds familiar, you're in good company. Most resolutions don't make it to spring. It's tempting to blame a lack of discipline, but I think the real problem is how we set them up.

**We aim at outcomes, not actions.** "Lose twenty pounds" or "get organized" describes where you want to end up. It doesn't say what you'll do on a Tuesday night when you're tired. A goal with no concrete behavior attached is just a wish. "Walk for twenty minutes after dinner" is something you can actually do today.

**We go too big, too fast.** January brings a burst of motivation, and we spend it all at once: a new diet, a new gym routine and a 5 a.m. alarm, all starting on the first. Motivation is a poor fuel source. It runs out, often within a couple of weeks, and when it does, a plan built for the most energetic version of you collapses under the weight of the real one.

**We treat one slip as total failure.** Miss a workout, eat the cake, skip a day of journaling, and suddenly the whole thing feels ruined. So we quit. But the streak was never the point. Someone who exercises four days out of seven for a whole year is far ahead of someone who managed seven out of seven for three weeks.

**We let the calendar pick the timing.** January 1 is an arbitrary date. Many of us arrive at it worn out from the holidays, short on money and facing the darkest weeks of winter. It's an odd moment to overhaul your life.

**We skip the "why."** A resolution that comes from guilt, or from what you think you *should* want, won't survive a busy week. The ones that stick are usually tied to something you genuinely care about.

So what works better? Make smaller promises. Attach them to things you already do, like stretching while the coffee brews. Expect to stumble, and plan to start again the next day without drama. And give yourself permission to begin in March, or June, or whenever you're actually ready.

Change doesn't need a fresh calendar. It just needs a plan small enough to survive an ordinary week.


# Person A

# Ruth (task: resolutions)
67, retired accountant, widowed, writes a blog mostly read by other retirees in her town.

What she thinks: resolutions fail because they're about improving yourself on your own, and nobody is waiting for you to show up. The ones that worked in her life were the ones where someone else was counting on her.

Real case: she resolved to walk more for years and never did. Then her neighbour Joan, recently out of hip surgery, needed someone to walk with, and they've walked every weekday at 9 for two years. "I'd never let Joan down. I let myself down all the time."

Also: she thinks a lot of resolution writing is aimed at young people trying to be productive, and retirees are trying to stay connected, not productive.

How she answers: longer, with small anecdotes; polite; sometimes goes off on a tangent.


# Person B

# Leah (task: resolutions)
ER nurse, 34, works rotating 12-hour shifts (days, then nights, changing every few weeks).

What she thinks: resolution advice assumes you have a regular schedule. "Same time every day" and "morning routine" are impossible for shift workers, nurses, warehouse staff, parents of newborns. Resolutions fail for a lot of people because the advice is written for 9-to-5 office workers.

Real case: tried a 6am gym habit three years running and failed every time by February because of rotation. What finally worked: anchoring it to an event, not a time: "the gym is the first thing after any shift ends, day or night." She's kept it 14 months.

Blog readers: mostly other nurses and healthcare workers, from her Instagram.

She dislikes generic advice and the word "simply".

How she answers: practical, warm, a bit wry; gives specifics.
