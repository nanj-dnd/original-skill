**Strongest objection**: One person leaving and then nobody else leaving is thin evidence that the 1:1s caused it, and a sceptical reader may say so. **Test**: ask two people on your team whether "one thing to change" has actually changed how they work. If they say yes, the post stands. If they shrug, cut the "nobody else has left" line.

In month four of my first year as a manager, one of my engineers resigned.

In her exit interview, she said she'd never received clear feedback on her work.

I'd thought everything was fine. That was the problem. "Fine" was my read of the situation, not something I had ever actually told her. It was a wake-up call.

So I changed how I run 1:1s. They're now weekly, each one has a written agenda, and every agenda has a section called "one thing to change."

The written part matters. Feedback no longer waits for a performance review or a crisis. It's on the page every week, so neither person walks out assuming things are fine when nobody has said so.

Nobody else has left since.

If I keep one lesson from this year, it's this: people don't know where they stand just because nobody is complaining. You have to tell them, regularly, in a form you can both look back at.

Thank you to my team for being part of the year I learned that.
