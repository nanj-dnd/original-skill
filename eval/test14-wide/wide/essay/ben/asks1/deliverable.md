# Thesis and Outline: Social Media and Democracy

## Thesis

**Social media has, on balance, been bad for democracy—not because it is uniquely good at spreading lies or hate, but because its dominant business model has dismantled the gatekeeping, shared-reality, and deliberative conditions on which democratic self-government depends, while handing authoritarian actors a cheaper toolkit for surveillance and manipulation than they ever had before. Its genuine democratic gains—lowering the cost of mobilization and giving voice to the marginalized—are real but have proven shallow, episodic, and easily reversed.**

Why this position: it's falsifiable, it engages the strongest counter-evidence (Arab Spring, #MeToo, Black Lives Matter), and it moves past the tired "misinformation" framing to a structural argument that fits a politics course better than a media-studies one.

## Outline (~1,500 words)

### I. Introduction (~175 words)
- Hook: the whiplash from 2011 ("Facebook revolutions") to 2016 (Cambridge Analytica, Brexit, Trump) to 2021 (January 6, Myanmar genocide findings).
- Frame the question: not "is social media good or evil," but what has it done to the *structural preconditions* of democracy—information gatekeeping, a shared factual baseline, and deliberation.
- Define scope: global, from roughly 2009 to present; "democracy" understood both as competitive elections and as the civic conditions that make them meaningful.
- State thesis.

### II. The strongest case for the defense: lowering the cost of collective action (~275 words)
Present the optimist case fairly before dismantling it.
- Mobilization: Arab Spring (Tunisia, Egypt), Hong Kong 2014/2019, Iran 2022, Black Lives Matter, #MeToo. Platforms collapsed the organizational costs that once required parties, unions, or churches.
- Voice: historically excluded groups bypass legacy media gatekeepers who ignored them.
- Accountability: citizen video (George Floyd), crowdsourced documentation of state abuse.
- Sources: Philip Howard & Muzammil Hussain, *Democracy's Fourth Wave?* (2013); Zeynep Tufekci, *Twitter and Tear Gas* (2017) — note Tufekci is useful on *both* sides.

### III. Why those gains are shallow: the "tactical freeze" (~225 words)
Pivot: the same features that make mobilization easy make it fragile.
- Tufekci's core argument: movements that skip the slow work of building organizations arrive at scale without the capacity to negotiate, adapt, or hold territory. Egypt 2011 → Sisi 2013 is the paradigm case.
- Networked protest produces moments, not institutions; democracy requires institutions.
- Evgeny Morozov, *The Net Delusion* (2011) on "cyber-utopianism."
- Transition: and the state learned faster than the protesters did.

### IV. The authoritarian learning curve (~250 words)
The global dimension most U.S.-centric essays miss.
- Seva Gunitsky, "Corrupting the Cyber-Commons" (*Perspectives on Politics*, 2015): regimes moved from blocking social media to *co-opting* it—flooding, astroturfing, surveillance, discrediting.
- Cases: China's Weibo/WeChat ecosystem; Russia's Internet Research Agency; Saudi Twitter infiltration; India's IT cells and WhatsApp-linked lynchings; Philippines under Duterte (Maria Ressa).
- Myanmar: Facebook's own-commissioned report (BSR, 2018) and Amnesty International (2022) on the platform's role in anti-Rohingya violence.
- Freedom House, *Freedom on the Net* (annual) — document the consistent decline in internet freedom since 2010.
- Key point: the technology is regime-agnostic, but the incentives favor those with resources to manipulate at scale.

### V. The core harm in democracies: collapsing gatekeepers and the shared baseline (~325 words)
The analytical heart of the essay.
- Democracies never ran on perfect information; they ran on *gatekeepers* (parties, editors) who filtered out the worst actors. Levitsky & Ziblatt, *How Democracies Die* (2018), on gatekeeping; social media lets candidates and movements route around parties entirely.
- Engagement-optimized algorithms reward outrage and novelty over accuracy. Vosoughi, Roy & Aral (*Science*, 2018): false news diffuses farther and faster than true news on Twitter—driven by humans, not bots.
- Fragmentation of the information commons: Andrew Chadwick, *The Hybrid Media System* (2017) on how legacy and social media now co-produce agendas, with the incentives flowing upward from platforms.
- Polarization: Bail et al. (*PNAS*, 2018) found exposure to opposing views on Twitter *increased* polarization—undercutting the naive "just show people the other side" fix.
- Honest caveat (strengthens credibility): the empirical literature is contested. Boxell, Gentzkow & Shapiro (*PNAS*, 2017) found U.S. polarization rose fastest among the demographic least online; Allcott & Gentzkow (*JEP*, 2017) found "fake news" exposure in 2016 was limited; the 2023 Meta/Science studies (Guess et al.; Nyhan et al.) found changing feeds had small short-term attitude effects. Lorenz-Spreen et al. (*Nature Human Behaviour*, 2023) systematic review: evidence of harm is strongest for polarization, populism, and trust, weaker for direct persuasion.
- Argue: the short-term experiments measure the wrong thing. The damage is cumulative and structural (trust, gatekeeping, incentive structures), not a one-election persuasion effect.

### VI. Counterargument and reply: "the problem is the business model, not the medium" (~125 words)
- Concede: a differently designed social media (chronological feeds, interoperability, public-interest governance) might not have these effects. The EU's Digital Services Act and researchers like Persily & Tucker (*Social Media and Democracy*, 2020) argue reform is possible.
- Reply: the essay question is historical and empirical—has social media *as it has actually existed* been good or bad? The counterfactual platform is not the one that shaped 2011–2026. Reform arguments actually concede the thesis.

### VII. Conclusion (~125 words)
- Restate: social media's democratic gift was cheap mobilization; its democratic cost was the erosion of the slow, institutional, gatekept conditions that turn mobilization into governance—and it has armed authoritarians more effectively than it has armed citizens.
- Close on implication: the question for the next decade is not whether to reverse the technology but whether democracies can rebuild gatekeeping functions (regulatory, institutional, or platform-level) before the trust deficit becomes self-reinforcing. Optional forward gesture: generative AI makes the gatekeeping problem more acute, not less.

## Notes for writing

- **Word budget** is approximate; Sections IV and V carry the argument and should get the most space.
- **Verify every citation** before submitting—I've listed real works from memory, but confirm page numbers, exact titles, and journal volumes in your library database.
- If your instructor prefers a narrower argument, drop Section IV and expand V—but the global angle is what makes this essay distinctive for a politics course rather than a comms course.
- Strongest single move in the essay: Section V's caveat paragraph. Engaging the null-result literature honestly and then explaining why it doesn't rescue the optimist case is what separates a B from an A.
