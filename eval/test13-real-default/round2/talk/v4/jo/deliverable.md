## Outline (5 minutes)

**1. Open — translate the question for the room (0:00–0:40)**
The paragraph below. Sets up the three meanings of "everything" and the three answers, so the structure does the work and nobody is waiting for a position.

**2. Every engineer? Yes. (0:40–1:40)**
- For the non-engineers: this isn't a specialist tool a few people use; it's the baseline now, and it's fine to assume anyone on the team is using one.
- What that buys: the parts of the job that are lookup and repetition get faster. The thing you'd have searched for, the change that touches forty files the same way, the unfamiliar library you'd have spent an hour reading up on.
- Keep this short. The room already believes it; spending time here costs you time where the actual question is.

**3. Every task? Yes, as a first pass. (1:40–2:50)**
- The honest middle, and where you'll spend the most care. Try it first on almost anything, including hard problems — a quick first version is useful even when it's wrong, because it shows you what you didn't ask for.
- "First pass" means it's an input, not an output. The assistant fails where the task depends on things it can't see: what the customer actually asked for, what broke the last time, what we decided not to build and why. Those live in people's heads and in this room, not in the code.
- So the harder the task, the more the engineer's work moves from writing to two other things: deciding what to ask for, and judging what came back. That's not less work. It's different work, and it's the part we're paid for.

**4. Every line? No. (2:50–4:00)**
- The rule in one sentence: the assistant never gets the last read.
- Why: a change nobody can explain is a change nobody can fix. Speed on the way in becomes slowness on the way out, usually at the worst moment.
- A concrete habit to offer: before you send a change for review, could you walk a colleague through why each piece is there? If not, you're not done, however good it looks.
- For the non-engineers: this is why the name on every change is still a person's, and why "why does it do that?" is still a question you ask an engineer, not a tool.

**5. Close (4:00–5:00)**
- Say what it means for each half of the room. Engineers: use these tools more than you do now, and read more carefully than you do now; both at once. Everyone else: expect faster first versions, and expect us to still be the ones who can explain them.
- Restate the three answers in one breath — every engineer yes, every task yes to start, every line no — and stop there.

## Opening paragraph

If you're not an engineer, here's what this question is really about. For the last couple of years there have been tools that will write code for you: you describe what you want, it writes a version, and you decide whether to keep it. The question on the table is whether we should be doing that for all of our work, and I've got five minutes to answer it. Here's the short version. "Everything" is doing a lot of work in that sentence. It can mean every engineer, every task, or every line that goes out the door, and those are three different questions with three different answers. Every engineer: yes. Every task: yes, as a starting point. Every line: no. And the no is the part worth the five minutes.
