# Should Engineers Use AI for Everything? (5-minute all-hands talk)

**Main point:** For most things, yes. But "everything" is where it gets interesting, and the part AI can't do is still the actual job.

**Audience:** the whole company (~30 people), so keep it non-technical. **Tone:** casual, a bit self-deprecating. **Length:** about 650–750 spoken words.

---

## Opening paragraph

> Hi, I'm [name], I'm on the engineering team, I've been here about eight months, and I'm up here because when someone asked who wanted to talk about AI coding tools, I didn't say no fast enough. So, full disclosure: I use AI for basically everything I write. The question I was given is "should engineers be using AI coding assistants for everything?" My short answer is: for most things, yes. But the word "everything" hides the interesting part, because the stuff AI *can't* do for me turns out to be most of what makes someone a good engineer. In the next five minutes I want to give you a clear picture of what this actually looks like day to day, so the next time you hear "AI writes the code now," you'll know which parts of that are true.

---

## Outline

### 1. Opening (0:00–0:45)
- The paragraph above.
- Main point to land: *yes for most things, and here's what the rest is.*

### 2. What it actually looks like (0:45–1:45)
*For the non-engineers in the room. Make it concrete instead of abstract.*
- Walk through one real task from your last week or two, start to finish: what you asked the AI, what it gave you, what you changed, and how long it took. **[Pick a normal, slightly boring task. Ordinary is more convincing than impressive.]**
- One-line way to describe it: it's less "the robot does my job" and more "I describe what I want, it writes a first draft, and I edit hard."
- Optional laugh line: show or read out a prompt you actually typed. Real prompts tend to be funny because they're so casual.

### 3. Where it's genuinely great (1:45–2:45)
*Pick 2–3. Don't list them all.*
- **Repetitive code:** the boring setup and test code nobody wants to write.
- **Getting unstuck:** explaining an error message, or suggesting three ways to try something.
- **Learning a codebase fast:** asking "what does this part do?" instead of interrupting a coworker. **[If this is true for you, say so. Coming from someone eight months in, it's a strong point.]**
- The payoff for the company: small things get done faster, so more time goes to the parts that matter.

### 4. Where I don't hand it the wheel (2:45–3:45)
- **It's confidently wrong sometimes.** It doesn't say "I'm not sure." It just gives you something plausible. **[If you've caught it being wrong, describe it in one or two sentences. Even a small one works.]**
- **It doesn't know our context:** our customers, why we made past decisions, what we're trying to build next. That comes from people.
- **High-stakes code** (security, customer data, billing, anything that's hard to undo) gets extra human attention no matter who or what wrote it.
- **The rule I follow:** I'm responsible for every line I ship, whoever typed it. "The AI wrote it" is not an excuse.

### 5. The honest new-grad worry (3:45–4:20)
*This is the part only you can say well, so keep it.*
- A fair concern: if junior engineers use AI for everything, do they ever really learn?
- Your honest answer: the risk is real when you just accept what it gives you. It's useful when you make yourself understand what it wrote before you ship it. **[Say what you actually do, e.g., ask it to explain its code, or try it yourself first on things you want to learn.]**

### 6. So: everything? (4:20–5:00)
- Bring it back: *for most of the typing, yes. For the thinking and the judgment, no.*
- What it means for everyone else: engineering can move faster on routine work, but review and good judgment still matter, so if something seems off, ask.
- Closing line, a callback to the opening:
  > "So should engineers use AI for everything? Use it for everything you'd be happy to double-check, which turns out to be most things. The rest is still the job."

---

## Delivery notes
- **Slides (optional):** keep it to 3 or fewer: (1) the question, (2) a screenshot of a real prompt and its answer, (3) the closing line. Talking with no slides works fine too.
- **Timing:** rehearse once out loud with a timer. Five minutes goes fast. If you run long, cut section 3 down to one example.
- **Fill in the [brackets]** with your own experiences. Those few real details are what will make the talk stick.
