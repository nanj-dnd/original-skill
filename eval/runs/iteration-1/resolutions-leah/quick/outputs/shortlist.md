# Why most New Year's resolutions fail (quick mode)

Quick mode: no questions asked; working from the task alone.

## Fixed and open parts

- **Fixed:** ~400 words; a blog post; personal blog (first person is fine, but no invented personal anecdotes); any research cited must be real. The popular statistics ("80% fail by February", "only 8% succeed") have no traceable primary source, so the post does not use them.
- **Open:** the claim about why they fail, the angle, the examples, the structure, the assumed reader, and what the post asks the reader to do.

## What everyone gets

Main claim: resolutions fail because they are too vague and too ambitious and depend on willpower; the fix is SMART goals, small habits, tracking and self-compassion.

Choices it makes:
1. Thesis: the fault lies in how the goal is written (vague or too big) and in relying on willpower.
2. Hook: an untraceable stat ("80% of resolutions fail by February" / "only 8% succeed").
3. Structure: a listicle of 4 to 6 reasons, each with a tip (SMART, start small, habit stacking, accountability partner, be kind to yourself).
4. Examples: gym, diet, weight loss.
5. Reader: someone who wants to succeed this time and needs better technique.
6. Leaves out: what the resolution competes with for time, and the fact that the year it lands in is already full.

## Candidates

Banned (one thing only): the default's main claim, "they fail because they're vague/too big and rely on willpower; fix with SMART goals and small habits."

1. The premise is shaky: the famous failure stats have no traceable source, and the best-known longitudinal study (Norcross) found people who make resolutions do better than people who want to change but don't. (start: a fixed fact that sits badly with the default)
2. Resolutions fail because they are scored pass/fail on a 365-day contract; nothing else in life is judged that way, and half a habit is still a gain. (start: inverted default choice, the scoring rather than the person)
3. Resolutions are a ritual of hope, like a birthday wish; judging them by completion misreads what they're for. (start: the reader who would disagree)
4. Resolutions are decided in the least representative week of the year: time off, no routine, holiday food. You plan for a life you aren't living. (start: inverted default choice, when rather than what)
5. Every resolution is an addition to a year that is already full; nothing is taken out to make room, so the new thing loses to the old things that already own the time. (start: what the default leaves out)
6. "I resolved" is identity talk, so a single missed day disproves the identity and the whole thing collapses (the "what-the-hell" effect).
7. Resolutions are borrowed from the culture (gym, diet), so you have no personal stake in them.
8. An autopsy of my own last five resolutions, as a personal record. (start: the constraint they named, a personal blog)
9. The real failure point is week three, not January: nobody plans the boring middle once novelty runs out.
10. Resolutions are private contracts with no witness, so nothing is lost by dropping them.
11. Announcing a resolution gives you the satisfaction of having done it, so you do less (Gollwitzer et al. 2009, on publicly stated identity intentions). *Unlikely.*
12. Starting in January means starting when everyone else does, and quitting when everyone else does: the crowded gym in week one and the empty one in week six are social proof in both directions. *Unlikely.*
13. The failure rate is a feature: a yearly do-over gives you a dozen attempts per decade, and many lasting changes come after several failed attempts. *Unlikely.*

## Cuts

- **6 (identity / what-the-hell effect):** close to the default's "be kind to yourself after a slip"; reads as the default's self-compassion tip with a label on it.
- **7 (borrowed goals):** the default's "pick goals that matter to you", reworded.
- **8 (personal autopsy):** needs the user's real resolutions; in quick mode it would mean inventing personal facts for a post under their name.
- **9 (week three):** near-duplicate of 2 and of the default's "novelty wears off".
- **10 (no witness):** the default's "get an accountability partner", reworded.
- **11 (announcing reduces effort):** depends on a single study whose effect is contested and has replication questions; risky to build a 400-word post on.
- **13 (failure as a feature):** near-duplicate of 3 (both say judging by completion is wrong); 3 states it more cleanly.

No web search for prior work: this is a blog post, and its value doesn't depend on the angle being new to the world.

## Shortlist (picked for spread)

**A. The year is already full (candidate 5)**
Resolutions fail because every one is an addition to a schedule with no free space in it. Nothing is removed to make room, so the new habit loses to the old ones that already own the time. The fix is to name what you'll stop before naming what you'll start.
- **Only yours because:** nothing; this is available to anyone.
- **Strongest objection:** plenty of resolutions are removals ("quit drinking", "stop scrolling") and fail anyway.
- **Test:** has to survive that rebuttal. The answer: "quit" resolutions remove the act but leave the time slot and the need it served, so the gap fills back up. Supporting fact: Adams et al. (2021, *Nature*) found people systematically overlook subtractive changes when trying to improve things.

**B. You plan in the wrong week (candidate 4)**
The week between Christmas and New Year is the least representative week of the year: no commute, no routine, time to think. You write the resolution for that person, then January's real schedule shows up.
- **Only yours because:** nothing; this is available to anyone.
- **Strongest objection:** people who make resolutions in March fail too, so timing can't be the main cause.
- **Test:** predicts that resolutions set during an ordinary working week hold better than ones set over the holidays; that could be checked.

**C. The scorecard is the failure (candidate 2)**
"Failing" means not doing it every day for twelve months. Going to the gym 40 times in a year when you went 5 times last year counts as a failed resolution. The pass/fail framing produces most of the failure rate.
- **Only yours because:** nothing; this is available to anyone.
- **Strongest objection:** this redefines failure instead of explaining it, which dodges the question.
- **Test:** has to survive "so nothing ever fails?"; needs a clear line between partial success and real abandonment.

**D. The premise is wrong (candidate 1)**
The "most resolutions fail" stats are untraceable, and the longitudinal data suggests resolvers outdo people who want to change but don't commit. The interesting question is why we enjoy believing they fail.
- **Only yours because:** nothing; this is available to anyone.
- **Strongest objection:** the user asked why they fail, and a post that disputes the premise may not be what they want.
- **Test:** depends on Norcross et al. (2002, *Journal of Clinical Psychology*) holding: about 46% of resolvers vs 4% of non-resolvers reported success at six months. That would need checking before publishing.

**E. Everyone quits together (candidate 12, unlikely)**
January starts are collective, so they are also collective quits: the crowded gym of week one and the empty one of week six are social proof in both directions.
- **Only yours because:** nothing; this is available to anyone.
- **Strongest objection:** explains gym resolutions well and private ones (reading, saving money) poorly.
- **Test:** predicts that resolutions shared with no group (a private reading goal) last longer than ones done in public places that empty out; checkable against gym attendance data.

## Recommendation and pick

Developing **A (the year is already full)**. It answers the question the user asked (unlike D), applies to every kind of resolution (unlike E), and gives the reader a concrete action that differs from the default's SMART-goal advice, which B and C don't. It also has the cleanest real fact behind it (Adams et al. 2021) and doesn't lean on the untraceable failure statistics.

Note: no answer from the user shaped any of these, so every "only yours" is "nothing". Quick mode can move the post off the default, but not toward this person's own view.
