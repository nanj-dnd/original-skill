**What everyone gets**
Claim: "AI coding assistants are a powerful tool, not a replacement for engineering judgment — use them for the right things." Choices a pasted prompt makes: angle is the balanced "it depends" middle; examples are boilerplate, tests and docs on the yes side, architecture, security and anything you don't understand on the no side; structure is hook, what they're good at, where they fail, three rules to live by; reader is addressed as "we engineers" even though it's an all-hands; tone is upbeat and a little TED-ish, opening with a productivity stat or a "a year ago none of us..." line.

Four questions, reply like `1b 2a 3c 4b`:

1. **Your actual stance.** Default is "great tool, don't use it for everything."
 a) That's roughly my view
 b) Stronger yes: we should be defaulting to it far more than we do
 c) More cautious: we're already leaning on it too much
 d) Genuinely undecided; I want to frame the question for the room, not settle it

2. **One real thing from our team to build on.** Default has none.
 a) A moment AI-written code caused a bug, incident or wasted review time (tell me what happened)
 b) A moment it saved real hours on something (what, and roughly how long)
 c) A number: share of PRs or code touched by assistants, hours saved, review load, anything measured
 d) Nothing to hand; write around it

3. **Who's in the room and how it should sound.** Default talks to engineers in an upbeat keynote tone.
 a) Plain and conversational, engineers are the real audience
 b) Plain, but pitched so sales, product and leadership follow and care
 c) More measured and formal, leadership is listening for a position
 d) Blunt or funny; the room expects you to have an opinion

4. **What you want to happen when you sit down.** Default ends with three generic rules.
 a) Propose a specific norm or policy (tell me what)
 b) Open a debate and invite pushback
 c) Share what you've learned, no ask
 d) Not sure yet
