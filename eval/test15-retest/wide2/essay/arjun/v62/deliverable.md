## Thesis

Debates over whether social media has been good or bad for democracy treat two different technologies as one. Algorithmic public feeds rank content by predicted engagement and so shape what citizens see. Encrypted messaging apps rank nothing; they move content through trusted personal networks that no platform, journalist or court can observe. India's 2018 WhatsApp lynchings show that the second architecture can produce deadly political harm with no algorithm involved. They also show that the most effective response was a design change to sharing mechanics, the limit on forwarding to five chats, rather than content moderation. Social media's democratic effects follow specific design features, so they must be judged, and governed, platform by platform.

## Outline (about 1,500 words)

**I. Introduction (about 200 words)**
- Open with the 2018 wave of killings in India. Rumours about child kidnappers spread through WhatsApp forwards, and mobs attacked strangers. The most cited example is Rainpada, Maharashtra, in July 2018, where five men were killed.
- WhatsApp's response that month was to limit forwarding to five chats in India, label forwarded messages, and remove the quick-forward button beside media.
- The problem: most of the "social media and democracy" debate is built on Facebook, Twitter and YouTube feeds, and the Indian case doesn't fit that model.
- State the thesis.

**II. Two architectures, two kinds of democratic risk (about 250 words)**
- *Public algorithmic feeds:* one-to-many broadcasting, ranked by predicted engagement, publicly visible, and moderated by the platform. Their democratic risk is distorted salience, meaning which issues, actors and emotions get amplified.
- *Private encrypted messaging:* many small groups, unranked, spread through personal trust, and invisible to the platform. Their democratic risk is unaccountable mobilization: rumours carry the credibility of the friend or relative who forwarded them, and no one outside can correct them.
- Conclude that the two architectures need different diagnostics and different remedies, and that the rest of the essay tests this claim.

**III. Case study: WhatsApp in India, 2018 (about 350 words)**
- How the rumours spread. Banaji and Bhat (2019) find that misinformation travelled through trusted groups and landed on existing prejudice against outsiders and minorities. Ranking played no part.
- Why feed-style fixes could not work. End-to-end encryption rules out downranking, content labels based on reading the message, and takedowns. The platform cannot see what is being said.
- The design response, all aimed at the mechanics of sharing rather than at content:
  - the five-chat forward limit and the "Forwarded" label in India (July 2018);
  - the five-chat limit applied globally (January 2019);
  - "frequently forwarded" messages limited to one chat (April 2020).
- What the limit did. De Freitas Melo et al. find that forwarding limits slow viral spread but do not stop it.
- Lesson: in messaging systems, friction is the main lever. That lever acts on speed, not on what people believe.

**IV. Contrast: what changing a feed does and doesn't do (about 300 words)**
- Huszár et al. (2022) show that Twitter's ranking algorithm amplified some political content more than a chronological timeline did. In feeds, design visibly shapes exposure.
- Guess et al. (2023) moved Facebook and Instagram users to chronological feeds during the 2020 US election. Exposure and time spent changed, but polarization did not measurably shift over three months.
- What this means for the thesis:
  - Feed harms sit in exposure and salience, so remedies are about ranking.
  - Messaging harms sit in velocity and trust, so remedies are about friction.
  - A single "social media" verdict misses both.

**V. Objection and response (about 250 words)**
- *Objection:* the lynchings were caused by communal tension, low institutional trust and political actors, not by an app. Design is downstream of society.
- *Response:* concede that design does not create grievances. Argue that it sets how fast and how far a grievance becomes action, and that this is the part of the problem a democracy can actually regulate.
- *Complication:* the Indian government's 2021 IT Rules demanded that platforms be able to trace a message to its originator, and WhatsApp challenged this in court as a threat to encryption. This shows that the wrong diagnosis can produce a remedy that itself harms democratic rights, here privacy and free expression. That strengthens the case for design-specific analysis.

**VI. Conclusion (about 150 words)**
- Replace "good or bad" with a set of design questions to ask of any platform:
  - Does it rank content?
  - Can outsiders see what spreads?
  - What does it cost a user to share?
- Restate the comparison: feeds need accountability for how they rank, and messaging apps need friction that does not break encryption.
- Close by returning to the 2018 forward limit. It was a small change to a single button, and it shows where democratic leverage over these platforms actually lies.
