## Angle

- **Angle:** Social media is good at the half of democracy that challenges power and bad at the half that settles disputes. So its net effect depends on which half a country is short of.
- **Built from:** 1d (mixed, but with a sharper reason than "double-edged"), 3d (democracy kept broad, split into two functions), 4a (formal, cited). For 2d I chose Belarus, Brazil and Taiwan to move off the usual US and Arab Spring cases.
- **Strongest objection:** The split between contesting and settling is tidy but may not hold. Polarisation and refusal to accept results have causes that predate social media, such as partisan TV, inequality and elite rhetoric. Platforms may just carry these, not cause them. **Test:** before drafting, read Guess et al. (2023) and ask whether your Section IV can survive its finding. If you can't answer it in two sentences, soften "weakens settlement" to "speeds up whatever unsettles a society."
- **Other direction:** Argue it's mostly good, treating the settlement failures as growing pains that institutions will adapt to, as they did after earlier media shifts.

## Thesis

Social media has been neither simply good nor simply bad for democracy. It has strengthened democracy's capacity for contestation (exposing power, mobilising opposition, breaking official monopolies on information) while weakening its capacity for settlement (getting citizens to accept outcomes they lost). Where contestation is the scarce good, as under closed or semi-authoritarian regimes, the gains dominate. Where settlement is scarce, as in polarised established democracies, the costs dominate. Taiwan's experience suggests the imbalance comes from platform design, not from networked communication itself.

## Outline (about 1,500 words)

**I. Introduction: what democracy actually asks of citizens (about 175 words)**
- Open with Przeworski's definition: democracy is a system in which parties lose elections (*Democracy and the Market*, 1991). It needs people willing to challenge power, and also people willing to lose.
- Name the two functions: contestation and settlement. Note that "good or bad" debates usually measure only one of them.
- State the thesis.

**II. Contestation: what social media made cheaper (about 300 words)**
- Case: Belarus, August 2020. After the disputed election, the Telegram channel NEXTA became a main coordinating channel for mass protest under a regime that controlled broadcast media.
- Theory: Tufekci, *Twitter and Tear Gas* (2017). Networked movements can mobilise huge crowds fast, without years of organisation.
- Point: in this function, social media did what democrats hoped it would.

**III. The hidden limit inside the success (about 200 words)**
- Also from Tufekci: the movements that scale fast often lack the structures to negotiate, decide or turn a crowd into lasting power.
- Belarus again: the regime survived. Social media helped people contest the outcome but gave them no way to settle it.
- This links the two halves: the same features that speed up contestation fail at settlement.

**IV. Settlement: what social media made harder (about 300 words)**
- Case: Brazil, 8 January 2023. After Lula's 2022 victory, supporters of Bolsonaro who rejected the result stormed the Congress, Supreme Court and presidential palace. The protests were coordinated partly through messaging-app groups.
- Theory: losers' consent (Anderson et al., *Losers' Consent*, 2005). Democracies hold together when the losing side accepts the result, and closed groups that reinforce one side's story make that acceptance harder.
- Point: in an established democracy, contestation was already plentiful. What ran short was settlement.

**V. The deciding variable: which good is scarce (about 175 words)**
- Bring the cases together. The same tools strengthened democracy where contestation was blocked and strained it where settlement was fragile.
- This is the sharper answer to "good or bad?": the verdict depends on the country, and the essay can say which countries.

**VI. Design, not destiny: Taiwan (about 200 words)**
- Case: vTaiwan and the Pol.is platform, used in national consultations such as the 2015–16 debate on regulating Uber.
- Pol.is shows users the statements that win agreement across opposing groups, not the ones that drive the most engagement. This is a working example of networked tools built for settlement.
- Point: the imbalance follows from engagement-driven design choices, which suggests it can be changed.

**VII. Objection and reply (about 100 words)**
- Objection: Guess et al. (*Science*, 2023) found that switching Facebook and Instagram users to chronological feeds during the 2020 US election did not measurably reduce polarisation.
- Reply: a three-month change for individual users tests individual attitudes, not settlement at the level of a whole society. Concede that the causal link is still contested.

**VIII. Conclusion (about 50 words)**
- Social media gave democracies a louder voice for objection without a matching tool for agreement.
- The better question is not whether it has been good or bad, but whether it can be redesigned to help settle disputes as well as start them.
