# Joan Is Waiting on Her Front Step

Every January, for more years than I'd care to count, I made the same resolution: walk more. And every year, I didn't. Nobody said a word, because nobody noticed.

Most of what you read about resolutions says the trouble is how we set them. Too vague, too big, not written down. Say "twenty minutes a day" instead of "walk more," the advice goes, and you'll be away. I'm not so sure. You can make a promise to yourself as precise as a railway timetable, but when it's pouring on a Tuesday morning, the only person you let down by staying in is you. And if you're anything like me, you're very good at forgiving yourself. I've had plenty of practice.

Then, two years ago, my neighbour Joan had her hip done. She needed someone to walk with while she got her strength back. We've walked every weekday at nine o'clock ever since. Rain, cold, it doesn't matter. I didn't turn into a more disciplined person. The only thing that changed is that Joan is waiting on her front step.

That's the whole secret, as far as I can tell. I'd never let Joan down. I let myself down all the time.

You'll sometimes see this dressed up as getting an "accountability partner," as if it were another trick for keeping yourself in line. That isn't quite it. Joan wasn't there to keep an eye on me. She needed someone, and it happened to be me. The walk stuck because it was never really about my resolution at all.

I've noticed, too, that most of this resolution business is written for young people trying to get more done before breakfast. At our age, most of us aren't trying to be more productive. We're trying to stay connected. And it turns out those aren't separate things. The connection is what got me out of the door.

So I won't give you a list of tips this January. You'll have seen plenty already. I'd only ask one question: if you didn't turn up, who would notice? If the honest answer is nobody, it may not last past the first wet week. But if there's someone nearby who could do with a hand, or a bit of company, or a reason to get out of the house, you might find you've made a resolution that isn't yours to break.

I should add that Joan is now quicker than me, which I find a bit cheeky. But there we are.


# Person A

# Tomás (task: resolutions)
47, former smoker for 15 years, quit on his 4th serious attempt (6 years smoke-free). Works as a high-school chemistry teacher.

What he thinks: "most resolutions fail" is the wrong frame. Most attempts fail, and that's normal; his first three quits weren't failures, they were how he learned what triggered him (the first coffee, driving, his brother). He'd count attempts, not outcomes, and he's read that most successful quitters needed several attempts.

Real case: attempt 2 lasted 5 weeks and broke at a wedding. That's how he learned weddings and drinking were the trigger, and attempt 4 planned around exactly that.

Hates: "be kind to yourself" fluff, and anything that sounds like a self-help book.

Blog readers: friends, some former students, people from a quit-smoking forum.

How he answers: blunt, a teacher's habit of correcting framing.


# Person B

# Ruth (task: resolutions)
67, retired accountant, widowed, writes a blog mostly read by other retirees in her town.

What she thinks: resolutions fail because they're about improving yourself on your own, and nobody is waiting for you to show up. The ones that worked in her life were the ones where someone else was counting on her.

Real case: she resolved to walk more for years and never did. Then her neighbour Joan, recently out of hip surgery, needed someone to walk with, and they've walked every weekday at 9 for two years. "I'd never let Joan down. I let myself down all the time."

Also: she thinks a lot of resolution writing is aimed at young people trying to be productive, and retirees are trying to stay connected, not productive.

How she answers: longer, with small anecdotes; polite; sometimes goes off on a tangent.
