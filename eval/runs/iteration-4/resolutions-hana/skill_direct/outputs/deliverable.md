# Nothing Grows in January

Every January, plenty of people make a resolution, and by spring it's gone. The usual explanation is that they just give up. That's true as far as it goes, but I don't think willpower is the whole story. I think most resolutions fail because we plant them in frozen ground.

Ask a gardener what they do in January and you won't hear "start everything." You'll hear: order seeds, read the catalogues by the fire, sketch next year's beds on the back of an envelope, clean and sharpen the tools, maybe prune the apple tree on a dry day. January is for planning. Nobody sensible sows tomatoes outdoors in the first week of the year and then blames the tomatoes when nothing comes up.

Yet that is exactly what a New Year's resolution asks of us. We pick the darkest, coldest, most expensive stretch of the year, with short days, holiday bills and everyone worn out, and decide that this is the moment to start running, or eating better, or finally writing the book. Then when the new habit wilts by February, we call it a failure of character. It's closer to a failure of timing. The conditions weren't there. Of course people gave up.

Gardeners already know a better rhythm, because the garden enforces it. You plan in winter. You start seeds indoors in late winter, somewhere warm where you can keep an eye on them. You harden them off gradually, a few hours outside and then a few more, before they go into the ground. And you plant out in spring, after the last frost, when the soil is ready to take them. At no point do you expect a seedling to do everything at once on the coldest day of the year.

So instead of a resolution, I'd suggest using January the way the garden does. Write the plan. Decide what you want to grow this year, in your life as well as your beds, and what it will need. Then wait for your own last frost: lighter evenings, a bit more energy, the first afternoon you're out in the garden without gloves. Start then, and start the way you'd start a seed tray, not a whole border.

(If you garden in the Southern Hemisphere, you're reading this in midsummer, and I apologise. Pick your own spring.)

Some things will still fail. Some seeds never germinate, and some plans don't survive March. But I'd bet more of them take when they go into warm soil. Nothing grows in January, and there's no shame in not asking it to.
