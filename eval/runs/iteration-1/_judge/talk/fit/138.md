# Where Did the Time Go? (5-minute all-hands talk)

## Opening paragraph

Here's a question I can't answer, and I'd bet nobody in this room can: last quarter, how many hours did AI coding assistants save our engineers, and what did we do with them? We've spent a lot of energy arguing about whether engineers should use these tools for everything. Some of us use them all day; some of us won't touch them. But in all that arguing, nobody wrote down what we expected to get back. If these tools save time, that time went somewhere: more features, more bugs to clean up, or earlier evenings. Nobody chose which. And if they don't save time, we have no way of knowing. So I'm not going to tell you whether to use AI for everything. I'm going to argue that it's the wrong question, and that the right one belongs to everyone here, not just engineering.

## Outline

**1. Opening: the question nobody can answer (0:00–0:45)**
- The paragraph above. Pause after the opening question and let it sit.

**2. Why "for everything" is the wrong question (0:45–1:30)**
- The debate is framed as one engineer's choice, one task at a time: autocomplete here, a test file there.
- What matters isn't how fast someone types. It's where the team's time goes, and that's a company outcome.
- One line for the non-engineers: "This is about your roadmap, not our editors."

**3. What we actually know: less than either side claims (1:30–2:30)**
- In a 2025 randomized trial by METR, experienced open-source developers took about 19% longer on real tasks with AI tools, while believing they'd been about 20% faster. [Verify the figures before Friday.]
- Don't present this as proof the tools are bad. The tools and the people have changed since then, and other teams report real gains.
- The point: feeling faster isn't a measurement, and we haven't taken ours.
- Even when time is saved, it moves rather than disappears: less typing, more reading, reviewing and owning code someone (or something) else wrote.

**4. If there's time to spare, three places it can go (2:30–3:45)**
- **More scope:** ship more features. Product and sales will feel this first.
- **More care:** review, tests, paying down old problems, fewer 2am incidents. Support and customers feel this one.
- **More people:** time for juniors to learn the hard way on purpose, so we still have senior engineers in five years.
- All three are fine choices. The bad option is the one we're taking now: not choosing, so it defaults to whoever asks loudest.

**5. The ask (3:45–4:30)**
- Engineers keep choosing their own tools. No mandate, no ban.
- Each team picks one or two things to track for the next six weeks: time from start to ship, time PRs wait for review, how often work gets redone or reverted.
- Leadership and teams decide out loud where any time saved goes, even as a rough split.
- Enthusiasts and skeptics both report back. We want both stories.

**6. Close: back to the opening question (4:30–5:00)**
- "Today, none of us can say what these tools saved us or where it went. Give me six weeks, and at the next all-hands I'd like to answer that question with a number, and with a decision we made on purpose."


# Person A

# Priya (task: talk)
Engineering manager, 40-person fintech, 9 years in. Leads 7 engineers.

What she thinks: AI assistants are fine and she's not going to ban them. The thing nobody talks about is that the cost moved from writing to reviewing, and it landed on her two senior engineers. PR volume is up about 60% since spring; review time per senior went from ~4 to ~11 hours a week. She wants to propose a rule: the author owns every line, "the AI wrote it" is never an excuse in a postmortem, and PRs over 400 changed lines get split.

Real case: in August an AI-generated database migration silently dropped a NOT NULL constraint. It caused a reconciliation mismatch that took three weeks to trace. The engineer who merged it couldn't explain what the migration did.

Also true: new hires ramp about twice as fast now, and she's glad.

Audience: whole company (~40 people, half non-engineers: sales, ops, compliance). She does NOT want a pro/con talk; she finds those useless.

How she answers: direct, a sentence or two, gives numbers when relevant.


# Person B

# Sam (task: talk)
Mid-level backend developer at a regional bank, 4 years in. Was asked by their manager to give the talk.

What Sam thinks: mostly agrees with the standard view. Assistants are good for boilerplate, tests and unfamiliar APIs; you have to review everything; they make you faster but not better. Nothing contrarian.

One real constraint: compliance forbids pasting customer data or production logs into any external tool, and people keep almost doing it. Sam wants one minute of the talk to be a clear "here is the line" for that.

Audience: the bank's tech division, about 80 people, mostly engineers plus a few risk/compliance folks.

How Sam answers: short, often picks the first or most standard option, not very opinionated.
