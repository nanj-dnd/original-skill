## Angle

- **Angle:** Social media has made democracies better at contesting power and worse at settling disputes. It is neither good nor bad overall, because it strengthens one half of what democracy needs at the expense of the other.
- **Built from:** 1d (a balanced verdict, but sharper than "double-edged sword"), 2d (documented cases, none of the usual American ones), 3d (the standard benefits, harms and rebuttal shape, done well), 4b (university level, so it names scholarship).
- **Strongest objection:** The split between contesting and settling may be too tidy. Protest movements also need shared facts, and disinformation can serve those in power as easily as those challenging it, as in the Philippines in 2016. **Test:** Sort each case below into "helped contest" or "hurt settlement." If two or more won't sort cleanly, recast the thesis as a tendency rather than a rule.
- **Other direction:** Argue that the verdict depends on where a democracy starts. It may help emerging democracies more and harm established ones more, which is a split the review in Section II reports.

## Thesis

Social media has made democracies better at contesting power and worse at settling disputes. It has widened who can expose and challenge those in office. At the same time, it has weakened the shared facts and trust that let citizens accept outcomes they lose. The real question is which of those two capacities a given democracy can least afford to lose.

## Outline (about 1,500 words)

**I. Introduction (about 175 words)**
- Open with Nigeria's #EndSARS protests in October 2020. Online mobilisation pushed the government to announce the disbanding of the SARS police unit within days. That is the contesting half.
- Then the Lekki tollgate shooting on 20 October. A Lagos judicial panel later found that soldiers shot unarmed protesters, and the federal government rejected its findings. Nigerians still don't share one account of that night. That is the settling half.
- Define the two capacities in a sentence each:
  - *Contestation:* the ability to challenge and expose power.
  - *Settlement:* the ability to reach decisions and accept results on shared facts.
- State the thesis.

**II. What social media strengthened: contesting power (about 375 words)**
- *Cheaper mobilisation.* Ukraine's Euromaidan began in November 2013 after journalist Mustafa Nayyem's Facebook post calling people to the square.
- *Exposure from below.* #EndSARS spread testimony of police abuse that had circulated for years without official response.
- *Evidence across countries.* Lorenz-Spreen et al. (2023) reviewed about 500 studies of digital media and democracy. They found consistent links to more political participation and information-seeking, with benefits clearest in emerging democracies.
- *The limit within the strength.* Tufekci (2017) argues that easy mobilisation skips the slow organising that builds lasting capacity. Movements can fill a square quickly and then stall. This prepares the turn to Section III.

**III. What social media weakened: settling disputes (about 450 words)**
- *Shared facts.*
  - In India in 2017–18, rumours about child kidnappers spread on WhatsApp and preceded a series of mob killings.
  - In July 2018, WhatsApp responded by capping forwards in India at five chats.
  - Forwarded messages in private groups leave no common public record that anyone can correct.
- *Accepting outcomes.*
  - Brazil's 2018 election saw mass WhatsApp campaigns, including reports by Folha de S.Paulo of businesses funding bulk messaging.
  - Romania's Constitutional Court annulled the first round of the 2024 presidential election after a TikTok-driven surge and intelligence reports of a coordinated campaign.
  - Key point: the annulment itself split the public. When the information environment is doubted, even the remedy cannot settle the dispute.
- *Trust.* The same Lorenz-Spreen review links digital media to falling trust, rising polarisation and populism, most clearly in established democracies.
- *Mechanism.* Keep this to one short paragraph: reach without a shared record, and ranking that rewards what spreads. Don't let this become the essay; the cases carry it.

**IV. Objections and responses (about 325 words)**
- *"Correlation, not causation; polarisation predates platforms."*
  - Grant the evidence limits.
  - Note that where a platform is the main news channel, as with WhatsApp in India and Brazil, the platforms themselves redesigned features in response to harms.
  - That doesn't prove causation, but it shows the people with the most data acted as if the link was real.
- *"Settlement isn't doomed: look at Taiwan."*
  - vTaiwan used the Pol.is platform to surface points of agreement in regulating Uber.
  - The government answered rumours quickly through its "humour over rumour" approach.
  - Concede this, then sharpen the thesis. Settlement suffers under platforms' default designs. Taiwan shows it can be rebuilt, but only by deliberate and often state-backed effort. So the claim is about defaults, not destiny.

**V. Conclusion (about 175 words)**
- Return to Lekki. The same tools that ended SARS have left the country without an agreed account of what happened at the tollgate.
- Restate the verdict: a democracy gets louder and better at refusing, but worse at agreeing.
- End on what follows for judging any one country. Ask which capacity it is short of, rather than asking whether social media is good or bad.
