# Thesis

Social media has been bad for democracy—not because it gives more people a voice, but because the business model that distributes those voices rewards outrage over accuracy and tribal loyalty over deliberation. The platforms' real democratic gains (lower barriers to organizing, exposure of abuses, access for marginalized groups) are genuine but have been outweighed by the erosion of the shared factual baseline and the good-faith disagreement that self-government requires. The problem is fixable, but only by changing the incentives, not by hoping for better users.

# Outline (~1,500 words)

**I. Introduction (~150 words)**
- Hook: the same tools that fueled the Arab Spring and #MeToo also fueled January 6 and the Rohingya genocide in Myanmar.
- Frame the question honestly: "good or bad" depends on what democracy needs to function, not just on whether more people can speak.
- State thesis.

**II. The case for "good": what social media genuinely delivered (~300 words)**
- Lowered the cost of political participation and organizing (Arab Spring, Black Lives Matter, Hong Kong protests, mutual aid networks).
- Broke gatekeeper monopolies—stories that legacy media ignored (police violence captured on phones, #MeToo) reached the public.
- Gave marginalized communities direct channels to each other and to power.
- Concede these fully; the essay's argument is not that these gains are fake but that they came bundled with something worse.

**III. The core problem: engagement-driven distribution (~350 words)**
- Platforms are not neutral pipes; algorithmic feeds optimize for time-on-site and engagement.
- Content that provokes anger, fear, and in-group/out-group feeling reliably outperforms content that informs or invites reflection (cite research on moral-emotional language and virality; Facebook's own internal findings on reshares amplifying divisive content).
- Result: the "marketplace of ideas" has a thumb on the scale, and the thumb favors the most polarizing version of every argument.
- Key move: this is structural, not a matter of bad actors or dumb users. Even well-intentioned people get a distorted picture of what their fellow citizens believe.

**IV. Consequences for democratic function (~350 words)**
- *Collapse of shared facts:* democracies can disagree about values but need some common reality to negotiate over; parallel information ecosystems make compromise look like betrayal.
- *Affective polarization:* citizens increasingly dislike the other side as people, not just as policy opponents—which correlates with tolerating anti-democratic behavior from one's own side.
- *Vulnerability to manipulation:* cheap, scalable disinformation (foreign influence operations, coordinated inauthentic behavior, now AI-generated content) exploits the same incentives.
- *Degradation of representative accountability:* politicians learn that performing for the feed beats governing; the loudest get rewarded, the compromisers get primaried.

**V. Addressing the counterargument (~200 words)**
- "Correlation, not causation—polarization predates social media and has other drivers (cable news, partisan sorting, economic anxiety)." Acknowledge this; argue social media is an accelerant that removed the friction that previously slowed these trends, and that the cross-national pattern (similar effects in countries with different media histories) supports a causal contribution.
- "Blaming the tool lets citizens off the hook." Respond: holding individuals responsible is fine, but system design that reliably produces bad outcomes at scale is a design problem.

**VI. Conclusion (~150 words)**
- Restate: net harm, but the harm lives in the incentive structure, not in the idea of open networks.
- Brief gesture at what "fixing the incentives" could look like (chronological/choice-based feeds, transparency and auditing of recommendation systems, limits on virality mechanics, interoperability so users aren't locked in)—without turning this into a policy essay.
- Close on the stakes: democracy survived the printing press and broadcast TV by eventually building norms and institutions around them; the question is whether we'll do that again before the damage compounds.

---

A couple of notes: if you'd rather argue the "good" side, the same structure works inverted—lead with the harms in Section II, concede them, then make the case that participation and gatekeeper-breaking are the more fundamental democratic goods and that the harms are correctable. And at 1,500 words you'll want to pick two or three concrete examples per section rather than listing many—the outline above gives you options to choose from.
