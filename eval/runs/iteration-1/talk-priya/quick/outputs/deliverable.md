# Same as Everyone, on Purpose
*5 minutes, company all-hands. Target length is about 650 to 750 spoken words.*

## Outline

**0:00 to 0:45: Opening (paragraph below)**
Give the answer up front: yes, for almost everything. Then set up the real question: which parts of what we build are supposed to be different?

**0:45 to 1:45: Why "yes" is the right default**
- What these tools are good at: giving the most likely answer to a well-worn problem.
- Most of our code *should* be the most likely answer: login flows, migrations, test scaffolding, the standard settings screen. Being boring there is a feature.
- Concede the enthusiasts' point fully. Fighting the tool on solved problems is wasted effort.

**1:45 to 2:45: The catch**
- Every company we compete with has the same assistants, asks them the same questions and gets the same likely answers.
- Where we're paid to be different, the likely answer is by definition what everyone else ships.
- *[Insert one example: the thing customers name when they say why they picked us, and where it lives in the product or code.]*

**2:45 to 3:45: Where the line actually is**
- The line isn't easy versus hard work, or junior versus senior work. It's this: *should this be the same as everyone else's?*
- Two quick contrasts. *[One place where "same as everyone" is exactly right. One place where it would quietly erase what we sell.]*
- This is not only an engineering call. Product, sales and support often know better than engineering where we're different. Say so to the room directly.

**3:45 to 4:30: One ask**
- Each team writes down its "different on purpose" list: two or three places, no more.
- On those, a person writes the first draft. The assistant is used to critique it and find holes, not to produce it.
- Everywhere else, use it freely.

**4:30 to 5:00: Close**
Go back to the opening question. "Should engineers use AI for everything? For everything that should look like everyone else's. That's most things. Just not the things that are the reason any of us are here."

## Opening paragraph

I was asked to answer a yes-or-no question in five minutes: should our engineers be using AI coding assistants for everything? I'll give you my answer up front and spend the rest of the time earning it. Yes, for almost everything. These tools are very good at one thing: giving you the most likely answer to a problem. And most of the code we write *should* be the most likely answer. But that's also the catch. Every company we compete with has the same tools, asking the same questions, getting the same likely answers. So the question I want to leave you with isn't "AI or no AI." It's this: which parts of what we build are supposed to be different from everyone else's, and are we sure we still know which ones those are?
