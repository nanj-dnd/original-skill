# Your Year Is Already Full

Think of any resolution you've made: run more, read more, learn Spanish, cook at home, call your mother on Sundays. Every one of them is something to *add*.

That's the part nobody plans for. The year you're making resolutions about isn't empty. It's the same year you had in December: the same job, the same commute, the same evenings that vanish somewhere between dinner and bed. A resolution walks into that schedule and asks for three or four hours a week, and nothing moves out of its way. For a couple of weeks it squeezes in on novelty and good intentions. Then a deadline lands, someone gets sick, the weather turns, and the new thing loses a fight it was always going to lose, because the old things already own the time.

We usually call that a willpower problem. I think it's an arithmetic problem. You can't fit four new hours into a week that was already full.

This isn't just a January quirk. A 2021 study in *Nature* found that when people try to improve something, whether a design, an essay or a plan, they reach for additions and routinely overlook the option of taking something away. Resolutions are that same habit with a date attached. We ask "what should I start?" and almost never "what should I stop?"

Even the resolutions that sound like subtraction usually aren't. "No more wine on weeknights" removes the glass but leaves the 7 p.m. slot, the tiredness and whatever the wine was doing for you. A gap with nothing planned for it fills back up, usually with the thing you took out.

So try a different exercise this year. Before you write down what you'll start, write down what you'll stop to make room for it, specifically and by name, with the hours it frees. Not "less scrolling" but "no phone after 9, and that's the hour I read." Not "get fit" but "I'm leaving the Thursday committee, and Thursday evening is for running."

If you can't find anything to give up, that tells you something too: the resolution doesn't fit the life you actually have. Better to learn that on January 1st than to find out on February 10th and decide it was your fault.

Most resolutions don't fail because we're weak. They fail because we plan them as if the year had free space in it. It doesn't. You have to make the space before you can fill it.

# Person B

# Leah (task: resolutions)
ER nurse, 34, works rotating 12-hour shifts (days, then nights, changing every few weeks).

What she thinks: resolution advice assumes you have a regular schedule. "Same time every day" and "morning routine" are impossible for shift workers, nurses, warehouse staff, parents of newborns. Resolutions fail for a lot of people because the advice is written for 9-to-5 office workers.

Real case: tried a 6am gym habit three years running and failed every time by February because of rotation. What finally worked: anchoring it to an event, not a time: "the gym is the first thing after any shift ends, day or night." She's kept it 14 months.

Blog readers: mostly other nurses and healthcare workers, from her Instagram.

She dislikes generic advice and the word "simply".

How she answers: practical, warm, a bit wry; gives specifics.
