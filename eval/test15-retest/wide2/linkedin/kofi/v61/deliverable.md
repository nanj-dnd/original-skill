**Angle:** My worst mistakes as a first-year manager were things I left undone (feedback I didn't give, reviews I didn't finish), and the fix was structure, not good intentions.
**Built from:** 1b (three lessons, each tied to a real event), 2 (the exit interview, the 1:1 format, five months of coding and three slipped releases), 3c (candid), 4b (feedback leads).
**Strongest objection:** Opening with someone's departure can read as being about her more than about you, and on a team of six people will know who she is. **Test:** Read it as if you were her. If you'd wince, soften the opening or check with HR before posting.
**Other direction:** Lead with the bottleneck instead. "Three releases slipped because of me" is just as candid and involves no one else.

---

Four months after I became a manager, one of my engineers left. In her exit interview she said she'd never received clear feedback on her work.

I genuinely thought things were fine.

A year into managing a team of six in hospital IT, that's the moment I keep coming back to. Here are three things my first year taught me.

Thinking things are fine isn't the same as saying so. I assumed that if I wasn't raising problems, people knew where they stood. They don't. When a manager says nothing, the team hears nothing, not approval.

A written format beats meaning well. After she left, I started weekly 1:1s with a written agenda and a section called "one thing to change." Feedback now happens every week, not whenever I get round to it. Nobody else has left since. I can't prove that's the reason, but I'm not going back.

The work you were promoted for can become the work you're blocking. I kept coding for my first five months. My reviews ran late, and three releases slipped because of it. It's easy to hold on to the job you were good at. But a manager who is also the team's reviewer becomes a queue.

What links all three: my costliest mistakes this year weren't things I did. They were things I didn't do: feedback I didn't give, reviews I didn't finish.

That's what I'm working on in year two.

**Before you use it**
- Your former engineer is identifiable on a team of six. Check that quoting her exit interview, even paraphrased, is within your hospital's HR and social media policy.
- If you have a concrete sign the 1:1s are working beyond "nobody's left" (something one of your engineers said, or a change you made because of the "one thing to change" section), one sentence of it would strengthen the second lesson.
