# Thesis

Social media has been good for democracy when citizens needed to break in — to be heard, to organise, and to expose what those in power wanted hidden — and bad for the ordinary, slower work of keeping a democracy going: agreeing on basic facts, treating opponents as rivals rather than enemies, and accepting results. Because democracies spend most of their time on that second kind of work, the overall record so far is more bad than good. But the comparison between countries shows the damage follows from how platforms are used and governed, not from connection itself, so the verdict is not fixed.

# Outline (1,500 words)

## I. The question, and why "both" is a dodge (≈150 words)
- Open with India in July 2018: after rumours spread on WhatsApp led to mob killings, the company cut the number of times a message could be forwarded to five. Both a government and a platform decided the problem was speed itself.
- Point out that "social media is both good and bad" is true of nearly everything and tells you nothing. A real answer needs a test.
- Set the test: a democracy needs three things from its citizens — the ability to take part, a shared sense of what is true, and acceptance of each other and of results. Judge social media against each.
- State thesis.

## II. Test one — taking part: a clear win (≈300 words)
- Claim: on participation, social media has been good, almost everywhere.
- Evidence: Tunisia and Egypt 2011, where organising moved online faster than regimes could follow; Hong Kong 2019, run through Telegram and forums without leaders to arrest; Nigeria's #EndSARS protests in 2020, which grew on Twitter from scattered complaints into a national movement in days.
- Add the research: a 2023 systematic review in *Nature Human Behaviour* (Lorenz-Spreen and colleagues), which gathered hundreds of studies worldwide, found the most consistent positive effects of digital media were on political participation and access to information — and that these benefits showed up most clearly in newer democracies.
- Close the section: this is the gain nobody can seriously deny. Note where the essay is heading: the same review found the negatives mostly elsewhere, in the established democracies — which raises the question of what happens after the movement wins.

## III. Test two — knowing what's true: gatekeepers gone, nothing in their place (≈325 words)
- Claim: here the record turns. Removing gatekeepers let true stories out — phone footage of police violence that newspapers had ignored — but also let false ones travel faster than anyone could correct them.
- Evidence on speed: Vosoughi, Roy and Aral (*Science*, 2018) studied roughly 126,000 stories on Twitter and found false news reached people faster and further than true news, and that humans, not bots, were mainly responsible.
- Evidence on consequences: Myanmar, where a UN fact-finding mission in 2018 said Facebook had played a "determining role" in spreading hatred against the Rohingya, and Facebook later admitted it had been too slow; India's WhatsApp lynchings from Section I.
- Fair counterpoint, stated plainly: old media was also biased and sometimes wrong. Reply: a newspaper could be sued, corrected, or boycotted; a forwarded message cannot. The difference is not accuracy but the presence of any check at all.
- Transition: a democracy can survive some lies. The question is whether people still accept the same referees — which leads to the third test.

## IV. Test three — tolerating opponents and accepting results: the deepest harm, with a complication (≈325 words)
- Claim: social media has made it easier to see opponents as enemies and losses as theft.
- Evidence: Brazil, 8 January 2023, when supporters of the defeated president stormed Congress, the Supreme Court and the presidential palace after months of "stolen election" claims circulating on WhatsApp and Telegram; the Philippines, where Maria Ressa's reporting on Facebook-run networks attacking journalists and opponents earned her a Nobel Peace Prize in 2021 and a string of criminal cases at home.
- The complication, taken seriously: Boxell, Gentzkow and Shapiro (*PNAS*, 2017) found that in the United States polarisation grew fastest among people over 65 — the group least likely to be online. So social media cannot be the whole cause.
- Resolve it honestly: the harm is not that platforms invented hostility but that they made it participatory. On television you watch the other side being mocked; on a feed you join in, and are rewarded for it. This is also where the 2023 review's finding fits — the clearest declines in trust and rises in polarisation were in established democracies, the places with the most to lose on this test.

## V. What the pattern shows — and the case that breaks it (≈250 words)
- Step back: social media scores well on the test that matters at the start of a democracy (getting in) and badly on the two that matter for the rest of its life (shared facts, mutual acceptance). That is why the honest overall verdict is "more bad than good" rather than "mixed."
- Then the case that keeps the verdict from being final: Taiwan. Probably the most heavily targeted democracy in the world for foreign disinformation, it built volunteer fact-checking into its messaging apps (Cofacts, started by the civic-tech group g0v in 2016), set up an independent fact-checking centre, and had government offices answer rumours within hours in plain language and jokes. Its elections went ahead; trust held.
- The lesson: the same tool, the same attacks, a different outcome — because the society treated the platform as something to govern, not just to use.

## VI. Verdict (≈150 words)
- Answer the question directly: judged by what democracies need most of the time, social media has so far been bad for democracy — but bad in the way a fast road is dangerous, not in the way a weapon is.
- Return to the opening: the forward limit in India was the first admission that speed was the problem. Taiwan shows the second step — building the checks that newspapers once provided, in a form that moves as fast as the feed.
- Final line: the question is not whether to keep social media, but whether democracies will do the slow work of governing it before it finishes undoing the slow work they depend on.
