# Thesis

Social media has been neither good nor bad for democracy in itself. The same platforms have widened political participation and exposed abuses of power. They have also degraded the shared information and mutual tolerance on which self-government depends. Which of these effects dominated in a given country has depended less on the technology than on the speed and competence of the institutional response to it. Where courts, regulators and civil society adapted early, the gains were kept and the harms contained. Where they did not, the harms ran unchecked. The evidence that platform design alone determines democratic outcomes is weaker than both enthusiasts and critics assume. The verdict on social media is therefore still being written by the choices democracies make now.

# Outline (approximately 1,500 words)

## I. Introduction (about 175 words)
- **Opening contrast**: avoid the usual one.
  - Brazil: in January 2023, rioters attacked Congress and the Supreme Court, organised in part through Telegram and WhatsApp groups. This came months after Brazil's electoral court had expanded its own powers to order content removed.
  - Taiwan: in its January 2024 election, Taiwan faced a heavy, well-documented foreign disinformation campaign and still produced a result all sides accepted.
  - The point: same platforms, roughly the same period, different outcomes.
- **Definitions**:
  - "Good for democracy" means strengthening participation, accountability and the capacity to resolve disagreement peacefully.
  - "Bad" means weakening them.
- **Thesis and roadmap**: the real gains, the real harms, then what decides between them.

## II. The case that social media has been good for democracy (about 300 words)
- **Participation**: social media lowered the cost of organising and petitioning to near zero.
  - Use cases beyond the usual protest movements.
  - Armenia 2018: Nikol Pashinyan's Facebook live streams helped drive the "velvet revolution".
  - South Korea 2016–17: online organising fed the candlelight protests.
  - Both ended in peaceful transfers of power.
- **Accountability from below**: phone footage and open-source investigation gave citizens and journalists evidence that once only states had.
  - Example: Bellingcat's work on the downing of MH17.
- **Access and participation, measured**:
  - Lorenz-Spreen, Oswald, Lewandowsky and Hertwig reviewed several hundred studies worldwide (Nature Human Behaviour, 2023).
  - They found digital media consistently associated with higher political participation and news consumption.
  - The associations lean more positive in emerging democracies.
- **Transition**: these gains are real and recurrent. The question is whether they are offset.

## III. The case that it has been bad (about 350 words)
- **The same review's other finding**:
  - It finds digital media associated with lower institutional trust, higher polarisation and stronger populist support.
  - These associations are concentrated in established democracies, so the harms fall hardest where democracy was already working.
- **Information quality**: false claims travel through closed messaging apps where no fact-checker or editor can see them.
  - India, 2017–18: WhatsApp rumours of child abduction drove a wave of lynchings.
  - WhatsApp had more than 200 million Indian users at the time.
- **Polarisation and tolerance**:
  - Allcott, Braghieri, Eichmeyer and Gentzkow (American Economic Review, 2020): Americans paid to deactivate Facebook for four weeks before the 2018 midterms became less polarised on policy issues and reported higher wellbeing.
  - Bail et al. (PNAS, 2018): Republicans paid to follow a bot sharing opposing views on Twitter became more conservative, not less.
  - So the problem is not only echo chambers but how people encounter disagreement.
- **Concentrated private control**: a handful of firms make the moderation and ranking decisions that shape national debate. Their owners can change policy overnight, with no democratic input.
- **Transition**: both sections are true, so weighing the lists cannot settle "good or bad". The question becomes what made one side win in a given place.

## IV. Synthesis: the balance is set by institutional response (about 450 words)
This is the essay's argument, built on three cases. Each shows the same technology producing a different outcome.

- **Taiwan**:
  - After 2018 it built several defences:
    - Cofacts, a civic fact-checking bot on the LINE messaging app.
    - A government policy of rapid, often humorous rebuttal of rumours.
    - Media-literacy teaching in schools.
  - The 2024 election absorbed heavy interference without a contested result.
  - Civil society and government built the response together. The platforms did not.
- **India**:
  - After the lynchings, the government pressed WhatsApp. The company limited forwarding, first to five chats, then (in 2020) to one chat at a time for messages that had already been forwarded many times.
  - WhatsApp reported that the first limit cut forwarded messages by about a quarter and the second cut highly forwarded messages by about seventy percent.
  - The design change worked, but only after deaths and only once a government demanded it.
- **The European Union and the United States**:
  - The EU's Digital Services Act has applied to the largest platforms since 2023. It requires risk assessments, researcher access to data and transparency.
  - The United States has passed no comparable federal law and relies largely on platforms governing themselves.
  - The 2023 Meta election studies in Science and Nature (Guess et al.; Nyhan et al.) matter here. Moving American users to a chronological feed for three months reduced their time on the platform. It produced no measurable change in polarisation or political attitudes.
  - The lesson: changing one platform setting did little on its own. The surrounding information environment matters more.
- **General point**: the technology sets what is possible; institutions set what is likely. Where democracies treated social media early as something to govern, the harms were bounded. Where they left it to private firms, they were not.

## V. Objection and reply (about 125 words)
- **Objection**: the argument is circular. Countries with strong institutions resist most shocks, so the essay may be measuring prior democratic health, not response.
- **Reply**: the cases are chosen to break that link.
  - Taiwan is a young democracy under direct external threat, and it contained the harm.
  - The United States is one of the oldest continuous democracies, and it largely did not.
  - Brazil gave its courts sweeping removal powers and still suffered an attempt to overturn an election.
  - What separates them is not age or wealth. It is whether the response was fast, came from civil society as well as the state, and built citizens' resilience instead of only removing content.

## VI. Conclusion (about 100 words)
- Restate without repeating: the verdict on social media is not a property of the platforms. It is a record of what democracies did about them.
- Close on the implication: if the institutional response sets the balance, "good or bad" is not a finding but a task. The countries that handled it well acted before the damage, not after.
