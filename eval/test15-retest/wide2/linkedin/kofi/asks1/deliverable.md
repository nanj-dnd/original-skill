A year ago, after seven years as a senior engineer, I became the manager of the team I used to be part of.

Four months in, one of my engineers resigned. In her exit interview, she said she had never received clear feedback from me.

I had genuinely thought things were fine. That was the problem. I'd mistaken the absence of hard conversations for the absence of problems. She deserved clarity, and I didn't give it to her.

So I changed how I run 1:1s. Every week now has a written agenda and a section called "one thing to change." It builds honest feedback into the routine, before small issues turn into resignations. No one has left since.

The second lesson took longer. For five months, I kept coding because it felt productive and familiar. Meanwhile, my code reviews piled up, and three releases slipped while the team waited on me. They didn't need another engineer. They needed someone to clear the path.

Letting go of the work I was good at felt like losing my value. In reality, it was the start of doing my actual job.

If you're an engineer stepping into management, learn these lessons sooner than I did. Your team pays for every month you wait.
