**What everyone gets**

A pasted prompt returns: "Social media has been both good and bad for democracy, but on balance its harms now outweigh its benefits." The choices it makes:

- **Angle:** "mixed, but net negative" — a balanced-ledger essay that lands softly against.
- **Examples:** Arab Spring (good), 2016 election / Cambridge Analytica (bad), misinformation and filter bubbles, January 6.
- **Structure:** intro → benefits (access, mobilisation) → harms (polarisation, misinformation) → "it depends on regulation" → conclusion.
- **Reader:** a generic grader; **tone:** neutral, hedged, five paragraphs.

Four questions, reply like `1b 2a 3c 4d`:

**1. Stance.** The default lands on "mixed, net negative." Where do you actually sit?
a) Net negative, and willing to say so plainly
b) Net positive — the harms are real but overstated
c) The good/bad framing is the wrong question; it depends on which function of democracy (voting, deliberation, accountability, protest)
d) Genuinely undecided — want the essay to work it out

**2. Scope.** The default uses the US (2016, Jan 6) plus the Arab Spring. Which case do you want at the centre?
a) US politics
b) India or another large democracy you know
c) A specific non-US case (tell me which: Brazil, Philippines, Myanmar, UK, etc.)
d) Keep it global and comparative

**3. Your material.** Is there one thing you've actually seen, read, or lived that should anchor this — a moment on a platform, a specific study or figure, an election you followed, a group chat that went wrong?
a) Yes, I'll describe it in my reply
b) No — build from public cases and verified studies only
c) Use a specific study or dataset I name in my reply

**4. Reader and register.** Who's grading or reading this, and how should it sound?
a) University course — formal, argued, cited (tell me any course rules)
b) School assignment — clear and structured, less jargon
c) Op-ed / general reader — direct, opinionated, no citations
d) Something else (say what)
