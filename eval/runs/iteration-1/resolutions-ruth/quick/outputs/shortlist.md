# Why most New Year's resolutions fail: quick run

Quick mode: no questions asked. Everything below comes from the task alone ("~400-word blog post on why most New Year's resolutions fail, for my personal blog").

## Fixed and open parts

- Fixed: about 400 words; a blog post; the topic is resolution failure; any statistic used has to be real. The popular "80% of resolutions fail by February" figure has no solid source, so the post doesn't use it. The best-known study (Norcross et al., 2002, J. Clin. Psychol.) found roughly 46% of resolvers still succeeding at six months, against about 4% of people who wanted to change but didn't make a resolution. That sits awkwardly with "most fail", which is why candidate 1 below exists.
- Open: the claim (why they fail), the angle, the structure, the assumed reader, how the post ends.

## What everyone gets

Main claim: resolutions fail because people set vague, oversized goals and lean on willpower, which runs out by February. The fix is small, specific habits plus accountability.
- Thesis: willpower is limited and the goals are unrealistic.
- Hook: the "80% fail by February" (or "only 8% succeed") stat.
- Structure: hook stat, then 3-5 numbered reasons, then tips (SMART goals, start small, habit stacking, track progress, find a buddy), then an upbeat "be kind to yourself" close.
- Examples: gym in January, crash diets, Dry January.
- Assumed reader: someone who has already failed and feels guilty.
- Leaves out: what a resolution actually gives you on day one, and whether "most fail" is even true.

## Candidates

Starting points used: a fixed fact that sits badly with the default (1, 2), inverted default choices (3, 5, 7, 14, 15), the constraint they named (personal blog, 8), the reader who would disagree (6), and the timing/framing of the resolution itself (4, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13). No user answers or specific cases were available in quick mode.

1. The premise is shaky: the famous failure stats are folklore, and resolvers do much better than people who wait for "the right moment". The real failure is not resolving at all.
2. They "fail" because we score them as a binary streak: one missed day counts as total failure even though most of the behavior survived.
3. January is the worst month to start anything (dark, broke, worn out after the holidays). The date is borrowed, not chosen.
4. Most resolutions are penance for December rather than things we want, and nobody keeps up a punishment.
5. They're written by the person you are on December 31st (rested, reflective, off work) for the person you are on a Tuesday in February: a contract signed by someone else.
6. To the reader who scoffs at resolutions: non-resolvers don't change either, so a failed resolution still beats a smug non-attempt.
7. They fail because they're subtractions ("stop snacking"). You can't do a nothing, so there's no action to repeat.
8. Personal-blog version: a confession about the one resolution I've made five years running.
9. A resolution that comes back every year isn't failing. It's a value you keep re-affirming, and only the plan fails.
10. Resolutions are announced to everyone in the same week, so they become a performance; when the season ends, the audience leaves and so does the motive.
11. (unlikely) Resolutions don't fail, they expire. They're built for a six-week season, like Lent, and should be planned as one.
12. (unlikely) They fail because they succeed too early: the first two weeks pay out the real prize (feeling like a new person), and once that's delivered, the behavior has no job left.
13. (unlikely) A one-in-two hit rate on a free yearly experiment is a good result, and the post should argue we're scoring it wrong.
14. We live in weeks, not years, so a "year" goal has no unit to happen in.
15. The standard advice (SMART goals, start tiny) is itself a cause: it shrinks the goal until it's no longer worth wanting.

Banned: the default's main claim (unrealistic goals plus weak willpower, fixed by small specific habits).

## Cuts

- 7: this is the "start with additions" tip from the default, reworded.
- 14: the "be specific / break it down" advice, reworded.
- 8: needs a real resolution from the user's life. Quick mode gave none, and making one up under their name isn't acceptable.
- 2: overlaps with 1 and 13 (all three are about how failure is scored). Kept 1, which is the most concrete.
- 13: overlaps with 1. It's the same reframe without the fact behind it.
- 10: close to 12 (both say the motive was social or emotional and runs out). 12 has the sharper mechanism.
- 3, 4, 6, 15: these survive but sit close to shortlisted items (3 and 5 are both about timing, and 4 and 12 are both about the motive). Left out to keep the spread wide.

No web search: this is a blog angle, not an idea whose value depends on being new to the world.

## Shortlist

**A. The premise is wrong (1).** The "most fail" stats are poorly sourced. The best study found that nearly half of resolvers were still going at six months, against about 4% of people who didn't resolve. The real failure is never resolving.
- Only yours because: nothing; this is available to anyone.
- Strongest objection: it dodges the question the post was asked to answer, and readers who feel they failed won't feel seen.
- Test: the Norcross 2002 figures have to hold up when checked, and the post needs another source if the reader asks "what about the 80%?".

**B. Written by holiday-you for February-you (5).** The resolution is a contract signed by a different, better-rested person, and the person who has to keep it never agreed to the terms.
- Only yours because: nothing; this is available to anyone.
- Strongest objection: it's a cousin of the "unrealistic goals" default, with the person's state blamed instead of the size of the goal.
- Test: it predicts that resolutions made in an ordinary week (not a holiday) last longer. That's checkable, but I haven't verified it.

**C. They expire, they don't fail (11, unlikely).** Treat a resolution like Lent: a fixed season with an end date, then decide what carries over.
- Only yours because: nothing; this is available to anyone.
- Strongest objection: it redefines failure away instead of explaining it, and some goals (quitting smoking) don't work in seasons.
- Test: a reader trying it should find a deliberate six-week run easier to finish and evaluate than an open-ended vow.

**D. They succeed too early (12, unlikely).** The real prize, feeling like a new person, pays out in the first days. The actual results are months away and too gradual to notice. Once the feeling is spent, the behavior has nothing paying it, so it fades by week three.
- Only yours because: nothing; this is available to anyone.
- Strongest objection: it's a plausible psychological story, not an established finding, and some people really do want the result, not the feeling.
- Test: the prediction is that drop-off clusters right after the novelty fades (weeks two to four) rather than spreading evenly, and that people describe quitting as "it just stopped" rather than "I gave up".

**E. The recurring resolution is data (9).** If the same resolution comes back every year, the want is real and stable, and only the method keeps breaking. Keep the want and change the plan.
- Only yours because: nothing; this is available to anyone.
- Strongest objection: it's comforting rather than explanatory, and repeating a goal can also mean you don't want it enough.
- Test: readers should be able to name a resolution they've made three or more times. If most can't, the post has no reader.

## Picked (quick mode: no wait)

Developing **D, "they succeed too early"**. It answers the question the user actually asked (why they fail) with a mechanism the default doesn't have, and it changes the advice: plan for week three instead of shrinking the goal. A would argue with the user's premise, and C and E reframe failure rather than explain it. None of the five depends on anything the user said, because quick mode collected nothing.
