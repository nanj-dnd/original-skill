# The Resolution That Finally Stuck Wasn't Mine

For years, and I do mean years, I resolved every January to walk more. I'd buy new shoes, go out twice, and by the middle of February I'd be back to watching the weather from the kitchen window. The shoes did very well out of it. They stayed looking new for ages.

Two years ago my neighbour Joan had her hip done. Her physio told her she needed to walk every day, but she wasn't steady enough to go on her own. So I said I'd go with her.

We've walked every weekday at nine o'clock for two years now. Rain, cold, the lot. Last winter, when the paths were icy, we did laps of the garden centre instead, which the staff found very amusing.

I've thought a good deal about why that worked when all those Januaries of good intentions didn't. I wasn't suddenly more disciplined. What changed was simple: I'd never let Joan down. I let myself down all the time.

Once I'd noticed it, I saw it everywhere. In forty years of accounting I never missed a filing deadline, not once. Yet I couldn't keep a diary going past the third of January. The deadlines had people waiting at the other end of them. The diary had only me, and I've always been very good at excusing myself.

Much of what's written about resolutions seems aimed at young people trying to get more done: up at five, cold showers, a plan for every hour. But most of us here aren't trying to be more productive. We're trying to stay connected, to keep a reason to put our coats on and get out of the door. A resolution that has you meeting someone at nine o'clock does more good at our age than any amount of self-improvement.

That, I think, is why so many resolutions fail. It isn't that we're weak or lazy. It's that a promise made only to ourselves is very easy to break, because nobody is standing at the gate wondering where we've got to.

So this year, I'd gently suggest skipping the resolution about yourself altogether. Think instead of someone who could use you: a neighbour who's been poorly, a friend who's on her own more than she lets on, someone from church you haven't seen in a while. Make your promise to them.

You may find, as I did, that it's the first one you keep. And that you're the one who gets the most out of it.


# Person A

# Ruth (task: resolutions)
67, retired accountant, widowed, writes a blog mostly read by other retirees in her town.

What she thinks: resolutions fail because they're about improving yourself on your own, and nobody is waiting for you to show up. The ones that worked in her life were the ones where someone else was counting on her.

Real case: she resolved to walk more for years and never did. Then her neighbour Joan, recently out of hip surgery, needed someone to walk with, and they've walked every weekday at 9 for two years. "I'd never let Joan down. I let myself down all the time."

Also: she thinks a lot of resolution writing is aimed at young people trying to be productive, and retirees are trying to stay connected, not productive.

How she answers: longer, with small anecdotes; polite; sometimes goes off on a tangent.


# Person B

# Tomás (task: resolutions)
47, former smoker for 15 years, quit on his 4th serious attempt (6 years smoke-free). Works as a high-school chemistry teacher.

What he thinks: "most resolutions fail" is the wrong frame. Most attempts fail, and that's normal; his first three quits weren't failures, they were how he learned what triggered him (the first coffee, driving, his brother). He'd count attempts, not outcomes, and he's read that most successful quitters needed several attempts.

Real case: attempt 2 lasted 5 weeks and broke at a wedding. That's how he learned weddings and drinking were the trigger, and attempt 4 planned around exactly that.

Hates: "be kind to yourself" fluff, and anything that sounds like a self-help book.

Blog readers: friends, some former students, people from a quit-smoking forum.

How he answers: blunt, a teacher's habit of correcting framing.
