# Resolutions Don't Fail. Attempts Do.

Every January the same headline comes back: most New Year's resolutions are dead by February. Look at what that actually counts. Someone makes a promise on the first, gets checked a few weeks later, and if they've slipped, they're scored as a failure. One attempt, one verdict, case closed.

That's a strange way to measure anything that takes more than one try, and most real change takes more than one try. A survey that checks on you once in March can't tell the difference between someone who has given up and someone who is on attempt two of four.

I smoked for fifteen years. I quit for good on my fourth serious attempt, and I've been clean for six years. [If any of the four started on January 1, say so here.] By the January scorecard, I failed three times. I don't think the scorecard measured anything worth knowing.

Here's what those three "failures" were. Between them, they showed me what set me off: the first coffee of the day, driving, my brother. Attempt two lasted five weeks and ended at a wedding. Not because my willpower gave out that afternoon, but because weddings mean drinking, and drinking was a trigger I had never planned for. I found that out by walking straight into it.

So attempt four was planned around exactly that. My willpower wasn't any stronger the fourth time. What had changed was that I knew what would get me, and the only way I'd learned it was by getting got.

That's what the "resolutions fail" story leaves out. A broken attempt isn't the opposite of progress. It's where the next attempt gets its information. Throw it away as a failure and you start the next one exactly as blind as the last.

Smoking is the clearest case I know. Most people who quit for good needed several attempts first [UNCHECKED: verify this figure and its source before posting]. Score each of those attempts the way we score resolutions and quitting looks close to impossible. Count the people who eventually stopped, and it doesn't.

If you're reading this from the forum and you've just started again, I'm not going to tell you it doesn't matter. It does. But it isn't a verdict on you, and it isn't wasted unless you waste it. Work out where it broke: the hour, the place, who was there, what was in your hand. That's what you paid for.

Count attempts, not outcomes. Then plan the next one around what the last one showed you.
