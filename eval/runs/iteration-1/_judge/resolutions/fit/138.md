# Why Most New Year's Resolutions "Fail"

Every January the same number comes around: something like 80 percent of resolutions are abandoned by February. It shows up in news segments, gym ads and the first paragraph of posts like this one. Try to find where it comes from, though, and you mostly find other articles quoting it. I couldn't find a study behind it.

The studies that do exist tell a different story. In 2002 the psychologist John Norcross and his colleagues followed two groups: people who made New Year's resolutions, and people who wanted the same kind of change but didn't make one. Six months later, 46 percent of the resolvers were still succeeding. Of the non-resolvers, 4 percent were. A 2020 Swedish study of more than a thousand people found that 55 percent considered themselves successful a full year on.

That isn't a ritual that mostly fails. So why does it feel like one?

Because we grade resolutions pass/fail, and we grade them early. "Go to the gym four times a week" becomes a failure the first week you go twice, even though twice is two more than December. "Stop drinking" fails on the first glass, and the eleven dry days before it vanish from the record. Hardly any other goal in your life gets treated this way. Nobody calls a savings plan a failure because one month came in short.

That habit has a cost. Once a resolution counts as broken, there's no reason to keep going, so the partial version that was working gets dropped along with it. The real failure isn't the slip. It's the quitting that follows the slip, and the quitting comes from how we keep score.

The research hints at a way out. In the Swedish study, people whose resolutions were about doing something ("eat more vegetables") did better than people whose resolutions were about avoiding something ("stop eating sweets"): about 59 percent success against 47. An avoidance goal is pass/fail by design. One lapse and it's broken. An approach goal can be met a little, which leaves you something to keep.

So if you're making resolutions this year, keep score the way you would for anything else you care about. Count what you did, not whether you were perfect. Decide in advance that a bad week doesn't end the year. And when someone quotes that 80 percent figure at you, ask where it came from.


# Person A

# Ruth (task: resolutions)
67, retired accountant, widowed, writes a blog mostly read by other retirees in her town.

What she thinks: resolutions fail because they're about improving yourself on your own, and nobody is waiting for you to show up. The ones that worked in her life were the ones where someone else was counting on her.

Real case: she resolved to walk more for years and never did. Then her neighbour Joan, recently out of hip surgery, needed someone to walk with, and they've walked every weekday at 9 for two years. "I'd never let Joan down. I let myself down all the time."

Also: she thinks a lot of resolution writing is aimed at young people trying to be productive, and retirees are trying to stay connected, not productive.

How she answers: longer, with small anecdotes; polite; sometimes goes off on a tangent.


# Person B

# Tomás (task: resolutions)
47, former smoker for 15 years, quit on his 4th serious attempt (6 years smoke-free). Works as a high-school chemistry teacher.

What he thinks: "most resolutions fail" is the wrong frame. Most attempts fail, and that's normal; his first three quits weren't failures, they were how he learned what triggered him (the first coffee, driving, his brother). He'd count attempts, not outcomes, and he's read that most successful quitters needed several attempts.

Real case: attempt 2 lasted 5 weeks and broke at a wedding. That's how he learned weddings and drinking were the trigger, and attempt 4 planned around exactly that.

Hates: "be kind to yourself" fluff, and anything that sounds like a self-help book.

Blog readers: friends, some former students, people from a quit-smoking forum.

How he answers: blunt, a teacher's habit of correcting framing.
