# Willpower Doesn't Scale. Automation Does.

For three years I've tracked my New Year's resolutions in a spreadsheet. The running total: 11 resolutions, 2 that stuck.

That's a bad score, but the spreadsheet taught me something more useful. The two that stuck had the same shape, and every resolution that needed a decision from me every day failed.

## The two that stuck

The first was about saving. I didn't resolve to "spend less" or "be better with money." I set up an automatic transfer to savings on payday. One setup action, and after that the money moved whether I felt disciplined that month or not.

The second was fitness. Instead of resolving to go to the gym more, I cancelled the gym membership and bought a doorway pull-up bar. [Your detail: what made the bar work for you, e.g. where it hangs or when you use it.] Again: one action up front, then it ran on its own.

## Why the other nine failed

The rest of the list [your examples: two or three of the ones that failed] all asked me to make the right choice daily. A resolution like that isn't one decision. It's hundreds of them, and plenty land on days when you're tired, busy, or just not in the mood. Lose a few and the whole thing starts to feel broken, and then it quietly stops.

Willpower doesn't scale. You can't make the same good choice hundreds of times on effort alone. Automation does scale. A decision you make once, on a good day, keeps paying out on the bad ones.

## Turn your resolution into a setup action

Before you commit to anything this year, ask: does this need me to decide every day? If yes, can I swap it for one action that does the work for me?

- **Save more:** don't promise to spend less. Schedule a transfer that goes out on payday, before you see the money.
- **Invest more:** set up a recurring contribution, or raise your retirement contribution percentage once.
- **Stop impulse buying:** delete your saved cards from shopping sites and unsubscribe from store emails. One afternoon.
- **Cut subscriptions:** cancel them on January 1 rather than resolving to "watch your spending."

Not every goal converts cleanly. Some still need you to show up. But the closer a resolution gets to set-and-forget, the less it depends on who you are on a random Tuesday in March.

So this year, don't write a list of things to try harder at. Write a list of things to set up, and spend the first week of January setting them up.
