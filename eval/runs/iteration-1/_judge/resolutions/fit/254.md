# Why Most New Year's Resolutions Fail (and Why Two of Mine Didn't)

For three years I've logged every New Year's resolution I make in a spreadsheet. So far I've made 11, and 2 have stuck.

That's a bad record, but useful data: the two survivors share something the other nine don't.

The first was an automatic transfer to savings on payday. The second was cancelling my gym membership and buying a doorway pull-up bar. Each took a single setup action, then ran on its own.

Every resolution that failed needed a decision every day. Think of the usual ones: spend less, eat out less, work out after work. Each asks you to win the same argument with yourself 365 times. Eventually you lose one, then another, and it's over.

The usual explanation is that resolutions are too vague or too ambitious. Sometimes they are. But the deeper problem is that most of them run on willpower, and willpower doesn't scale. Automation does.

## The research agrees

This isn't just my spreadsheet. In a well-known 2001 study, economists Brigitte Madrian and Dennis Shea looked at a company that switched its 401(k) plan to automatic enrollment. Participation among new hires jumped from 37% to 86%. Same kind of employees, same paychecks, same goal. The only change was that saving no longer required a decision.

The gym tells the same story from the other side. Stefano DellaVigna and Ulrike Malmendier found that members on monthly contracts paid more than $17 per visit on average, when a 10-visit pass would have cost them $10 a visit. People sign up planning to decide to go, and then don't. My pull-up bar has no monthly fee, and it hangs in a doorway I walk through every day.

## How to fix your resolutions

This January, put each resolution through one test: **is there something I can do once that makes this happen without me?**

- **Saving more?** Don't resolve to "spend less." Set up a payday transfer so the money is gone before you see it.
- **Paying down debt?** Schedule an automatic extra payment instead of promising to put spare cash toward it.
- **Getting fit?** Drop any plan that starts with a commute. Put the equipment somewhere you can't avoid it.
- **Cutting subscriptions?** Cancel them today, in one sitting, instead of "keeping an eye on them."

If a resolution fails the test, rewrite it until it passes, or drop it. A resolution you have to remake every morning isn't one decision. It's 365 of them.

Two out of eleven isn't great. But I know exactly how the next ones are going to work.

What she thinks: resolution advice assumes you have a regular schedule. "Same time every day" and "morning routine" are impossible for shift workers, nurses, warehouse staff, parents of newborns. Resolutions fail for a lot of people because the advice is written for 9-to-5 office workers.
