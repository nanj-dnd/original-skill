1. Yes, and it's not on your list. "Most resolutions fail" is the wrong frame to begin with. Most *attempts* fail, and that's normal. It's how you learn. I'd count attempts, not outcomes. From what I've read, most people who successfully quit smoking needed several tries. So the post is about why the "resolutions fail" framing is wrong, not about why resolutions fail.

2. Smoking. I smoked for 15 years and quit on my 4th serious attempt. That was 6 years ago. The first three weren't failures, they showed me what triggered me: the first coffee, driving, my brother. Attempt 2 lasted 5 weeks and fell apart at a wedding. That's how I found out weddings plus drinking were the big one, so attempt 4 was planned around exactly that. Use the wedding.

3. Blunt. I teach high school chemistry, so I spend a lot of time correcting people's framing, and I write that way too. No "be kind to yourself," nothing that reads like a self-help book. If it sounds like a poster in a dentist's office, cut it.

4. Friends, some former students, and people from a quit-smoking forum. The forum people know the "how many attempts" stuff firsthand, so don't talk down to them.

5. Some practical stuff, but not a listicle. Basically: treat a failed attempt as data. Write down what broke it and plan the next attempt around that. That's the whole method.

6. One figure is fine if it's about how many attempts successful quitters needed, since that's the actual point. Skip the "80% quit by February" stat. That's exactly the framing I'm arguing against.
