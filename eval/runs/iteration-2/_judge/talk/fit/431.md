# "AI for Everything?" — 5-Minute All-Hands Talk

**Core message:** Use AI coding assistants for almost everything, but don't trust them with everything. The tool writes the code; the engineer still owns it.

**Timing guide:** about 650–750 spoken words. The minute marks below add up to 5:00, with a little slack.

---

## Opening Paragraph (≈0:00–0:45)

> Quick show of hands: who here has accepted an AI suggestion this week without reading every line of it? Keep your hand up if it was this morning. Okay, you can put them down. Nobody's in trouble. That's exactly why I wanted these five minutes. AI coding assistants have gone from a novelty to something most of us touch dozens of times a day, and somewhere along the way the question changed from "should we use these?" to "is there anything we *shouldn't* use them for?" My answer is that we should use them for almost everything, but we shouldn't hand them responsibility for anything. In the next few minutes I want to make the case for leaning in harder, be honest about where it goes wrong, and suggest one simple rule we can all work by.

---

## Outline

### 1. Opening: the show of hands (0:00–0:45)
- Use the hand-raise to get the room involved and admit, without blame, that this is already how we work.
- Restate the question: not "should we?" but "for *everything*?"
- Give the thesis early: **use it broadly, own it completely.**

### 2. Why "yes, for almost everything" (0:45–1:45)
- **It removes drudgery.** Boilerplate, test scaffolding, migrations, regexes, unfamiliar APIs, first drafts of docs. This is work nobody misses.
- **It shortens the path from idea to working prototype.** We can try three approaches in the time one used to take.
- **It helps with exploring and explaining,** not only with generating code: "Explain this legacy module," "What could break if I change this?"
- *Your example here:* one concrete win from our own team. A specific task, roughly how long it took before, and how long it takes now. One real story will land better than any industry statistic.

### 3. Where "everything" breaks down (1:45–3:00)
- **Confident and wrong.** Assistants produce plausible code that compiles, passes a quick look, and is subtly incorrect: edge cases, concurrency, off-by-ones, APIs that don't exist.
- **Security and sensitive data.** Generated code can bring in insecure patterns or outdated dependencies, and prompts can leak things that shouldn't leave the building. *(Mention our actual policy on approved tools and what may be pasted into them.)*
- **Skill atrophy, especially for early-career engineers.** If you never struggle with a problem, you never build the instincts you'll need to spot when the AI is wrong. Debugging intuition comes from doing.
- **The review bottleneck moves.** Writing code gets cheaper, but reviewing it doesn't. More code in PRs means more load on reviewers and a greater risk of rubber-stamping.
- **High-stakes areas need extra care:** auth, payments, data deletion, infrastructure, anything customer-facing and irreversible.

### 4. The rule: "You ship it, you own it" (3:00–4:15)
- **Use AI anywhere, but never merge code you can't explain.** If you can't walk a reviewer through it, it isn't ready.
- **Scale scrutiny to the stakes.** Light review for a throwaway script, deep review for production paths and sensitive systems.
- **Write the tests yourself, or at least read them carefully.** Tests the AI wrote to check its own code can share its blind spots.
- **For newer engineers:** sometimes solve it by hand first, then compare with what the AI produces. Treat it as a tutor, not a crutch.
- **Be open about it.** Mentioning in a PR that "AI drafted this part" is normal, not embarrassing. It helps reviewers know where to look.

### 5. Close (4:15–5:00)
- Come back to the opening: "Next time you're about to accept a suggestion without reading it, that's your cue."
- One-sentence takeaway: **"Let AI do the typing. You still do the thinking."**
- Point to a next step: a channel for sharing good and bad AI experiences, a lunch-and-learn, or the team's guidelines doc *(fill in whatever exists or is planned)*.

---

### Delivery notes
- **Keep it balanced.** You'll likely have both enthusiasts and skeptics in the room. Acknowledging both early ("it's already how we work" and "it can go wrong") keeps either group from tuning out.
- **Swap in real stories.** Have one win (Section 2) and, if you can share it without blaming anyone, one near-miss (Section 3). Specifics are what people remember.
- **Check the policy angle.** If the company has an official stance on AI tools, make sure Section 3 and the rule in Section 4 line up with it, or say clearly that this is your personal view.
- **If you run long,** cut the review-bottleneck point in Section 3 and the transparency bullet in Section 4 first.


# Person A

# Sam (task: talk)
Mid-level backend developer at a regional bank, 4 years in. Was asked by their manager to give the talk.

What Sam thinks: mostly agrees with the standard view. Assistants are good for boilerplate, tests and unfamiliar APIs; you have to review everything; they make you faster but not better. Nothing contrarian.

One real constraint: compliance forbids pasting customer data or production logs into any external tool, and people keep almost doing it. Sam wants one minute of the talk to be a clear "here is the line" for that.

Audience: the bank's tech division, about 80 people, mostly engineers plus a few risk/compliance folks.

How Sam answers: short, often picks the first or most standard option, not very opinionated.


# Person B

# Dana (task: talk)
CTO and co-founder of a 12-person startup (B2B scheduling software).

What she thinks: the question "should engineers use it for everything" is already settled at her company: they do. The interesting change is that the engineer's job is now writing the spec and the tests; the code is the cheap part. She wants the non-engineers in the room (sales, support, the other founder) to understand why engineers now spend most of their time writing tests and arguing about specs, and why a feature "that took a day" still needs a week before it ships.

Real case: last month they built a whole calendar-sync feature in one day by writing 140 tests first and letting an agent fill in the code. Then it took 6 more days because the spec was wrong about recurring events across time zones, which no test had covered.

Audience: all 12 people; 7 are not engineers. She wants it to be fun and short.

How she answers: fast, informal, a bit impatient with hedging.
