# Fast Isn't the Problem. Unchecked Is.
*5-minute all-hands talk: should engineers use AI coding assistants for everything?*

**The one idea:** Use AI tools for as much as they help. The tool doesn't know what matters to our customers, though, so for anything it writes, a named person has to check the parts it can't see. We also need to hear about it fast when something gets through.

---

## Opening paragraph (read word for word, about 50 seconds)

> Last quarter, a blind customer emailed our support team about our checkout form. The form had no labels for screen readers, so the fields were basically invisible to them. It took us two weeks to fix. And I found out about it from support, not from engineering. That's not a dig at anyone. It's just how the news traveled, and that's part of the story. That form was written with an AI coding assistant. So I've been asked to talk about whether engineers should be using these tools for everything. I'm a product designer, not an engineer, and I'm not going to tell anyone how to write code. But I think that one form answers the question better than any opinion I could give you, and the answer isn't "stop using AI."

---

## Outline

**1. Opening: the checkout form** (0:00–0:50)
- The paragraph above. Let the story make the case before you say what you think.

**2. The tool isn't the villain** (0:50–1:40)
- A person could have written that same form without labels. Plenty have.
- The AI did exactly what it was asked: it made a form that works. It just didn't know who has to be able to use it.
- So "should we use it for everything?" is the wrong question. The real question is: *when a tool writes it, what does a person still check?*

**3. Where I land** (1:40–2:50)
- Use it for whatever it makes faster. I'm not going to draw that line for engineers. They know their work far better than I do.
- But speed moves the risk. When code is quick to write, review becomes the part that protects customers.
- Accessibility is the example I happen to know. Engineers will have their own: things the tool can't know matter here.
- Shared ownership: design could have caught this too. Did our spec say the fields needed labels? *(Check this before Friday and say it honestly either way.)*

**4. The second gap: we heard late** (2:50–3:40)
- Two weeks to fix, and the news came through support, not through the team that built it.
- Faster tools mean we ship faster. If problems still take weeks to reach the people who can fix them, we're just making mistakes faster.

**5. Turn "be careful" into something concrete** (3:40–4:35)
- "Be careful" is what everyone says, and it doesn't change anything. Instead, one question for every team using these tools:
  - **"What doesn't this tool know matters to our customers, and who checks it before it ships?"**
- And one habit: when a customer reports a problem like this, it reaches the people who built the thing that same week.

**6. Close** (4:35–5:00)
- Come back to the customer: "A customer couldn't use our checkout, and the tool had no way of knowing. That's our job. Use the tools. Just make sure someone's looking at what they can't see."

## Delivery notes
- **Length:** about 650 words spoken, which fits 5 minutes at a relaxed pace. Read the opening word for word, then speak the rest from the bullets.
- **Slides:** not needed. If you want one, show only the question from section 5.
- **Tone:** calm and specific. The story carries the weight, so no need to dramatize it.

- **Check with support** that it's fine to tell the customer story. Keep out anything that could identify the customer.
