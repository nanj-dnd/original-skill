# When Code Gets Cheap: a 5-minute all-hands talk

## Opening paragraph (about 45 seconds spoken)

The question on the agenda is whether engineers should use AI coding assistants for everything. I'll answer it in one sentence and spend the rest of my five minutes on why it's the wrong question for this room. My answer: for most of what they write, yes. But look around. Most of the people here don't write code. And the part nobody puts on an all-hands slide is this: when writing code gets cheap, building things doesn't get faster in step with it. The slow part moves. It stops being the engineers and becomes the rest of us: the people who decide what's worth building, sign off on it, and tell customers it exists. So this isn't really a talk about engineers. It's about what happens to everyone else's job once they get faster.

## Outline (5:00 total, about 700 words)

**1. Opening: the wrong question for this room (0:00 to 0:45)**
- Give the answer up front, "for most of it, yes", so nobody spends the talk waiting for the verdict.
- Reframe: the interesting consequence lands on the non-engineers.

**2. The engineering answer, briefly (0:45 to 1:30)**
- "Everything" isn't free. The cost moves from writing code to reading it. Someone still has to understand every line that ships, and reviewing well is now a core engineering skill, not a chore.
- One sentence on the limit: whoever merges it owns it, however it was written.
- Keep this short on purpose. The engineers already know it, and everyone else doesn't need the detail.

**3. What changes when building is cheap (1:30 to 3:00)**
- The key line: *the queue moves.* If a prototype takes an afternoon instead of two weeks, the wait shifts to whatever comes after: a product decision, a design call, legal review, a customer conversation, a pricing answer.
- **[Insert one real example from our company]**: something an engineer built quickly that then sat waiting for a decision. One concrete case is worth more than any industry statistic here. Don't use a number you can't source.
- Name the trap: we'll be able to build more things than are worth building. The scarce skill becomes choosing, not making.

**4. What this asks of everyone else (3:00 to 4:15)**
Three asks, one sentence each:
- **Decide faster.** If engineering turns around a version in a day, a two-week sign-off becomes the whole timeline.
- **Write clearer requests.** When an assistant writes the code, the spec effectively *is* the code. Vague asks now get built exactly as vaguely as they were written.
- **Say no more often.** Cheap to build doesn't mean cheap to maintain, support, sell or explain.

**5. Close (4:15 to 5:00)**
- Come back to the question: "Should engineers use AI for everything? Mostly, yes. The better question is whether the rest of us are ready for them to."
- One concrete ask to leave the room with, for example: "Next time an engineer says 'I can have a version by tomorrow', be ready to make a decision by the day after."
- Stop there. Don't summarize.

*Speaker note: if engineers turn out to be most of the room, keep sections 1, 2 and 5 and replace sections 3 and 4 with what this means for how we train junior engineers.*
