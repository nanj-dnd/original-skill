**Angle**: Resolutions fail because they're private. Motivation isn't a tank you run down; it's something you borrow from whoever is going to ask about it, and most resolutions have nobody asking.

**Built from**: your "people lose motivation" (1), the Welsh streak and dinner check-ins with your daughter (2b), single reframe ending (3b), wry first person (4a).

**Strongest objection**: plenty of people keep private habits for years (runners, writers, people who floss), and plenty of public goals flop despite an audience. **Test**: list your last five attempts, stuck and fizzled. If the stuck ones all had a specific person checking in and the fizzled ones didn't, use it. If it's mixed, soften "the reason" to "a reason."

**Other direction**: the streak itself did the work, not your daughter; the app made the daily unit so small that quitting felt sillier than continuing.

**Why your resolution is already dying, and it isn't your fault**

Every January I used to think the problem was me. I'd pick something, go hard for three weeks, then quietly stop, and I'd file it under "lost motivation," as if motivation were petrol and I'd simply run out.

I don't think that anymore, because of Welsh.

About two years ago I started learning Welsh on an app. I am not Welsh. I have no plan to move to Wales. By any sensible measure this is the least necessary thing I do. And yet I'm on a 700-day streak, which is longer than I have kept up anything that wasn't a job or a mortgage.

The difference isn't that I wanted it more. I've wanted plenty of things more. The difference is that my daughter, who is twelve, does the same app, and at dinner we compare streaks. That's it. That's the whole system. Nobody is keeping score, except that we both obviously are.

Every other resolution I've made had something in common: nobody would have noticed if I stopped. I could have skipped the running and the world would have shrugged. I could have let the reading list rot and no one at dinner would have said, "So how's that going?" The resolution lived entirely inside my head, and my head is a terrible landlord. It evicts tenants the moment they get boring.

What we call losing motivation, I think, is mostly this: the goal was private, and private things have no cost of quitting. There's no awkward moment. There's no "oh, you stopped?" You just... don't do it one day, and then the next, and the resolution dissolves so gently you can't even say when it died.

Welsh didn't survive because I'm disciplined. It survived because a twelve-year-old would have asked, with the faint cruelty only twelve-year-olds can manage, why my number had gone back to zero. Three hundred days in, the lessons were genuinely dull. The dinner conversation wasn't.

So here's the reframe I'd offer instead of the usual advice about starting small and writing things down. Stop asking whether you want this enough. Ask who is going to notice if you stop. If the honest answer is "nobody," you don't have a resolution. You have a thought.

Find a witness. Preferably one who'll be a bit smug about it.
