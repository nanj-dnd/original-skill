## Opening paragraph

I was asked to talk about whether we should be using AI coding assistants for everything. My short answer is no, but I'm not going to spend my five minutes telling you which tasks to use them for. Most of you already have a better feel for that than any rule I could write, and where you use these tools is your call. There is one thing that isn't a call, though, and I'm going to be blunt about it because we keep getting close to it: no customer data and no production logs go into any external tool. Not one row, not a trimmed log line, not "just this once to debug it." I'll come back to that before I finish, and I'll say it more than once.


# Person A

# Marcus (task: talk)
Staff engineer, 300-person logistics company, does on-call rotation and runs the incident review.

What he thinks: the real risk is skill atrophy, not code quality. Over the last 6 months their median time-to-resolve for P2 incidents went from 38 to 61 minutes, and in incident reviews he keeps seeing engineers who can't read a stack trace through code they "wrote". He thinks juniors should do their first six months of debugging without an assistant, the way pilots still train on manual flying. He's fine with assistants for writing new code.

Real case: a 2am outage where the on-call engineer pasted the error into the assistant four times, got four different confident fixes, and it took Marcus 10 minutes to find it by reading the log.

Audience: whole company, but he mostly cares about engineers and their managers hearing it.

He is NOT anti-AI and gets annoyed if he's framed as a luddite.

How he answers: measured, slightly long, likes analogies from aviation (he's a private pilot).


# Person B

# Sam (task: talk)
Mid-level backend developer at a regional bank, 4 years in. Was asked by their manager to give the talk.

What Sam thinks: mostly agrees with the standard view. Assistants are good for boilerplate, tests and unfamiliar APIs; you have to review everything; they make you faster but not better. Nothing contrarian.

One real constraint: compliance forbids pasting customer data or production logs into any external tool, and people keep almost doing it. Sam wants one minute of the talk to be a clear "here is the line" for that.

Audience: the bank's tech division, about 80 people, mostly engineers plus a few risk/compliance folks.

How Sam answers: short, often picks the first or most standard option, not very opinionated.
