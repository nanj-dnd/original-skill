**Angle**: "Things seem fine" is what no feedback looks like from the manager's side — the quiet isn't trust, it's a gap the other person is filling in alone.
**Built from**: 1b and your month-four story (the exit interview line); 3b keeps it a reflection to your network rather than tips; 4a keeps it plain.
**Strongest objection**: One person leaving doesn't prove the pattern — maybe she had other reasons and feedback was the polite one to give. **Test**: before posting, ask two current reports, point blank, whether they know where they stand with you. If both answer without hesitating, soften the claim; if either pauses, you're right.
**Other direction**: Write it from her side — a post about what it's like to work for a manager who says nothing — and let the reader figure out it's you.

In month four of being a manager, one of my engineers quit.

In her exit interview she said she had never once received clear feedback on her work.

I genuinely thought things were fine.

That sentence is my whole first year. Not the stuff I expected to learn. This.

I wasn't holding feedback back on purpose. I just didn't have much to say that felt worth saying, so I didn't say it. From my side, that looked like trust. From her side, it was silence. She filled the silence with her own guesses, and then she left.

What I got wrong wasn't a hard conversation I avoided. It was all the ordinary weeks where nothing was wrong, so I said nothing, and "nothing" was the message.

It changed how I run my weekly 1:1s. The main shift: I stopped assuming that if I had nothing to say, there was nothing to say.

I don't have a list of lessons from year one. I have one, and it cost the team an engineer to teach it to me. Writing it down so it at least cost something less than that for someone else.
