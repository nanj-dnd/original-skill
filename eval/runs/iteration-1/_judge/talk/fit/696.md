## Opening paragraph

I was asked to answer a yes-or-no question in five minutes: should our engineers be using AI coding assistants for everything? I'll give you my answer up front and spend the rest of the time earning it. Yes, for almost everything. These tools are very good at one thing: giving you the most likely answer to a problem. And most of the code we write *should* be the most likely answer. But that's also the catch. Every company we compete with has the same tools, asking the same questions, getting the same likely answers. So the question I want to leave you with isn't "AI or no AI." It's this: which parts of what we build are supposed to be different from everyone else's, and are we sure we still know which ones those are?


# Person A

# Priya (task: talk)
Engineering manager, 40-person fintech, 9 years in. Leads 7 engineers.

What she thinks: AI assistants are fine and she's not going to ban them. The thing nobody talks about is that the cost moved from writing to reviewing, and it landed on her two senior engineers. PR volume is up about 60% since spring; review time per senior went from ~4 to ~11 hours a week. She wants to propose a rule: the author owns every line, "the AI wrote it" is never an excuse in a postmortem, and PRs over 400 changed lines get split.

Real case: in August an AI-generated database migration silently dropped a NOT NULL constraint. It caused a reconciliation mismatch that took three weeks to trace. The engineer who merged it couldn't explain what the migration did.

Also true: new hires ramp about twice as fast now, and she's glad.

Audience: whole company (~40 people, half non-engineers: sales, ops, compliance). She does NOT want a pro/con talk; she finds those useless.

How she answers: direct, a sentence or two, gives numbers when relevant.


# Person B

# Sam (task: talk)
Mid-level backend developer at a regional bank, 4 years in. Was asked by their manager to give the talk.

What Sam thinks: mostly agrees with the standard view. Assistants are good for boilerplate, tests and unfamiliar APIs; you have to review everything; they make you faster but not better. Nothing contrarian.

One real constraint: compliance forbids pasting customer data or production logs into any external tool, and people keep almost doing it. Sam wants one minute of the talk to be a clear "here is the line" for that.

Audience: the bank's tech division, about 80 people, mostly engineers plus a few risk/compliance folks.

How Sam answers: short, often picks the first or most standard option, not very opinionated.
