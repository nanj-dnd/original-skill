## Thesis

Asking whether social media has been good or bad for democracy treats two different architectures as one. On public algorithmic feeds, the harm runs through what the platform chooses to amplify, so ranking can be audited and changed. On private encrypted messaging, the harm runs through trusted personal networks that the platform cannot see, so the only design levers are friction and the only lasting remedies sit offline. India's WhatsApp lynchings, and the limited effect of the 2018 forwarding cap, show that democracies need to regulate social media mechanism by mechanism rather than issue a single verdict.

## Outline (about 1,500 words)

**I. Introduction (about 200 words)**
- Open with the July 2018 child-kidnapping rumours in India and the killing of five men in Rainpada, Dhule district, weeks before WhatsApp limited forwarding to five chats.
- Point out that no algorithm ranked those messages. They moved from phone to phone through family and community groups. A verdict built on "the algorithm" cannot explain this case.
- State the thesis, then preview the three mechanisms and the counterargument.

**II. Mechanism 1: Amplification on public algorithmic feeds (about 250 words)**
- On Facebook, YouTube, X and Instagram, reach is decided by the platform's ranking, so harm here is a matter of what gets amplified.
- Evidence that ranking shapes political exposure: Huszár et al. (2022) on algorithmic amplification of politics on Twitter.
- Complicate it with Guess et al. (2023). Switching users to chronological feeds changed what they saw but did not measurably reduce polarisation. Ranking matters, but it is not the whole story.
- Democratic point: because the content is public and the ranking can be audited, it can be governed by transparency rules, researcher access and changes to ranking.

**III. Mechanism 2: Trusted relay on private encrypted messaging (about 350 words; the core section)**
- How WhatsApp works: there is no feed or ranking. Messages spread by forwarding, and they borrow credibility from the person who sent them, a relative or neighbour rather than a stranger.
- India case: child-kidnapping rumours circulated as images, voice notes and video clips in local groups (Banaji & Bhat, 2019). Garimella & Eckles (2020) found that images carry much of the misinformation in Indian political WhatsApp groups.
- Why the feed-era toolkit fails here. End-to-end encryption means the platform cannot see, fact-check, label or down-rank content, and outside researchers cannot audit it.
- Democratic point: the same privacy that protects dissidents and journalists also lets rumours travel without anyone seeing them. The harm comes from the design, not from moderation failures.

**IV. Mechanism 3: Mechanisms determine remedies (about 300 words)**
- The July 2018 forwarding limit was friction, not content moderation. WhatsApp slowed sharing without reading messages.
- Its effect was limited. Melo et al. (2019) found that forwarding limits delay spread but do not stop viral content from reaching most of the network.
- The Indian state reached for the other lever, the traceability requirement in the IT Rules 2021, which demands identification of a message's "first originator". WhatsApp challenged it in the Delhi High Court as incompatible with encryption.
- Argument: remedies built for feeds either do nothing in encrypted apps or require breaking encryption, which trades one democratic good (privacy, safe dissent) for another (information integrity). This is why one verdict cannot cover both architectures.

**V. Counterargument and rebuttal (about 250 words)**
- Counterargument, stated fairly in two parts. First, the distinction is collapsing: large groups, Channels and cross-posting from public platforms make WhatsApp look more like a broadcaster. Second, Banaji & Bhat show the violence followed existing caste, religious and class prejudice, so blaming design misplaces the cause.
- Rebuttal to the first: the blurring is a matter of degree. Wherever content is encrypted and passed on through personal trust, the levers available still differ.
- Rebuttal to the second: concede it, and use it. The mechanism framing explains which pre-existing vulnerabilities each design activates, and why private-messaging harms need offline remedies such as policing, local institutions and media literacy, rather than platform fixes alone.

**VI. Conclusion (about 150 words)**
- Restate: the useful question is which mechanism, which harm and which lever.
- Implication for policy: regulate feeds through ranking transparency and audit. Treat encrypted messaging through friction plus state and civic capacity, not by weakening encryption.
- Close by returning to Rainpada: a design change came after the deaths, and the forwarding cap alone could not have prevented them.
