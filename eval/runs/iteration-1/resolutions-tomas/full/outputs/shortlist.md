## Shortlist

The five survivors are picked to differ from each other and from the default.

**A. Count attempts, not outcomes** (from 1 + 2 + 6)
"Most resolutions fail" grades the wrong thing. Most *attempts* fail, for smoking and for everything else worth doing, and that's normal. What matters is whether the person gets there. The person's record is the one to keep.
- **Only yours because:** this is your answer 1, nearly word for word. Your four attempts, six years clean, are the proof.
- **Strongest objection:** "Count attempts" can sound like a reframe that makes any outcome look fine. A skeptic says you've just moved the goalposts.
- **Test:** CDC data (Babb et al., MMWR 2017): about 55% of US adult smokers made a quit attempt in a year, but only about 7% quit successfully that year. Yet the US has more former smokers than current ones. Also, the "80% fail by February" figure has to turn out to have no traceable study behind it.

**B. Failure buys the information the advice assumes you have** (from 3 + 4 + 5) **(recommended)**
Every guide says "know your triggers." Nobody can, in advance. You learned the first coffee, driving and your brother by quitting and relapsing, and the wedding by relapsing at one. Attempt 4 worked because the earlier three were spent finding out what would get you. Will had nothing to do with it.
- **Only yours because:** your answers 2 and 3: the trigger list, the five-week attempt that broke at a wedding, and "I knew what would get me."
- **Strongest objection:** some people quit on the first try, cold turkey, with no reconnaissance. So failure isn't *required*.
- **Test:** the rebuttal it has to survive is that first-try quitters exist. The post has to claim "most", not "all". The supporting fact to check is Chaiton et al. (BMJ Open, 2016), whose estimates of attempts before success ran from about 6 to over 100, with about 30 as their main figure.

**C. Successes hide their failures** *(unlikely)* (from 10)
You're six years clean, and nobody asks how many tries it took. Ex-smokers tell the last attempt as the whole story. That makes the success look like it happened first try, and everyone still trying looks like a failure by comparison.
- **Only yours because:** the six years plus the three attempts nobody sees. It depends on you saying the quiet part.
- **Strongest objection:** it's more about storytelling than about why resolutions fail. It could read as a side point stretched to 400 words.
- **Test:** a prediction you can check. Ask a few ex-smokers you know how many serious attempts they made. If most say "one", the idea is wrong.

**D. The ritual's flaw is scarcity, not ambition** (from 7 + 8)
If quitting takes several attempts, a once-a-year attempt turns a few months of trying into several years. The resolution itself works: Norcross found resolvers succeeded at roughly ten times the rate of people who only meant to change. What fails is waiting for the next January to try again.
- **Only yours because:** partly nothing; anyone could make the scheduling argument. Your attempts (presumably not all on January 1) make it concrete.
- **Strongest objection:** nothing stops anyone retrying in February. The scarcity is a habit, not a rule.
- **Test:** Norcross, Mrykalo & Blagys (2002, *Journal of Clinical Psychology*): about 46% of resolvers vs. about 4% of non-resolvers were successful at six months. This needs checking before use.

**E. The headline makes itself true** (from 9)
Call a slip "failure" and people stop counting. Researchers have a name for it (the abstinence-violation effect: one cigarette, so I've blown it, so I'm a smoker again). The "most resolutions fail" statistic isn't just mismeasured. It's part of the reason people stop at attempt one.
- **Only yours because:** your answer 2 ("a failed attempt is data") turned around. The statistic teaches people to treat data as a verdict.
- **Strongest objection:** it's hard to show a blog-post statistic changes anyone's behaviour. The causal claim is bigger than the evidence.
- **Test:** the abstinence-violation effect comes from Marlatt & Gordon's relapse-prevention work (1985). The post can't claim more than that it's a described pattern.

**My recommendation: B.** It's an argument with your story as the evidence, which is the shape you asked for. It goes straight against the tips genre ("you don't fix failing with tips") without becoming self-help. And it lets the forum readers count their relapses as findings, not verdicts. A's claim ("count attempts, not outcomes") can open it.
