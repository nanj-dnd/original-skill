**The person who made the resolution isn't the one who has to keep it**

Resolutions are written by a very specific author: the late-December version of you. That person is rested, a little sentimental, surrounded by people who seem to have their lives together, and looking at a blank calendar. They are not the person who has to get up on a Tuesday in February and do the thing.

That's the whole problem, and it's why most of the usual explanations miss. We're told resolutions fail because they're too vague, or too big, or because willpower runs out. All true, sometimes. But people keep perfectly vague, enormous commitments all the time when they actually want them. Nobody needs a SMART goal to keep seeing someone they've fallen for.

The resolutions that die are usually about a result, not an activity. "Learn Spanish" means "be the kind of person who speaks Spanish." "Read more" means "be the kind of person who reads." The image is attractive. The flashcards are not. And a resolution is, in practice, a promise to do the flashcards, every day, long after the image has faded.

So the test is simple. Picture the activity on an ordinary, slightly bad day. Not the first day, when novelty carries you, and not the imagined last day, when you're fluent. Day forty. Raining. You're tired. Do you want to do it, or do you only want to have done it?

If it's the second, the resolution is a wish wearing a plan's clothes. It will fail, and the failure will feel like a character flaw, when really it was a mismatch: one version of you signed a contract, and a different version got handed the bill.

The ones that stick tend to look unimpressive from the outside. They're things you were already drifting toward, half-started, slightly embarrassed to call a goal. They don't need January because they don't need a fresh start; they just need permission to keep going.

Which suggests a strange question to ask before writing anything down this year. Not "who do I want to become?" but "what have I already been doing, badly and inconsistently, that I'd like to do a little more?" It's a smaller question. It gets kept.
