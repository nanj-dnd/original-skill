# Thesis

Whether social media has been good for democracy depends on what democracy is for. If it is for letting people who were previously ignored enter the public argument, the platforms have plainly helped: Black Lives Matter moved a demand from a Ferguson street into the national agenda in days, without a party, a newspaper or a donor. But getting in the door is not the same as being heard or staying in the room. The same decade shows attention won online decaying fast, counter-movements and impostors using the identical tools, and few of the movement's demands becoming law. Social media has been good for democracy at the entrance and unproven at the table, and the honest verdict for now is a clear gain in who speaks and an open question about who is listened to.

# Outline (≈1,500 words)

**I. Open on the case, not the question (≈175)**
- August 2014, Ferguson: the shooting of Michael Brown reaches national attention through residents' and activists' tweets before cable news arrives. The #BlackLivesMatter hashtag already exists (2013, after the Zimmerman acquittal) but this is when it becomes a movement.
- Name the yardstick the essay will use: democracy as participation — who can put something on the public agenda and be answered. State briefly why that yardstick is fair (it is what the old gatekeeper system most visibly failed at) and what it leaves out (debate quality, trust), to be picked up in V.
- Thesis.

**II. Who got to speak (≈325)**
- Before: police killings of Black Americans were covered, if at all, as local crime stories; the agenda was set by editors and officials.
- After: Freelon, McIlwain and Clark's *Beyond the Hashtag* (2016), the largest study of the movement's Twitter use, found activists used the platform to pull mainstream journalists and politicians toward their framing (policing, not "riots"), and largely succeeded.
- Pew's 2018 work on activism in the social media age: the hashtag used tens of millions of times over five years, and Black users far more likely than white users to call these platforms important for getting elected officials to pay attention.
- Point of the section: this is participation in the strict sense — new entrants setting the agenda — and it is a real democratic gain, not a concession to be moved past.

**III. Who got heard, and for how long (≈350)**
- 2020 as the peak: after George Floyd's death, protests in thousands of US towns, the largest in the country's history by most estimates, coordinated and documented almost entirely on platforms. Public support for the movement jumps.
- Then decay: polling support falls back within months and keeps sliding into 2021–23. The George Floyd Justice in Policing Act passes the House and dies in the Senate; Minneapolis voters reject replacing the police department in 2021. Real local changes (chokehold bans, body-camera rules) are modest set against the scale of the mobilisation.
- Zeynep Tufekci's argument in *Twitter and Tear Gas* (2017): networked movements reach scale in weeks but skip the organisational capacity (leadership, negotiation, discipline) that older movements built over years, so they can't convert attention into bargaining power. BLM fits this closely.
- Point: on the participation yardstick, voice was gained; influence was rented, not owned.

**IV. The same door is open to everyone (≈300)**
- Counter-mobilisation: #BlueLivesMatter and allied content travelled on the same feeds with the same mechanics, and often faster; being first is not being loudest.
- Impersonation: the Senate Intelligence Committee's reports (2019) on Russia's Internet Research Agency documented fake BLM-aligned pages ("Blacktivist" among them) built to inflame and discredit the real movement.
- Frame these as participation harms, not a separate "misinformation" section: a system that lets anyone in also lets in people whose purpose is to crowd out or counterfeit the voices that just arrived.
- Point: the platforms' democratic good and their democratic harm are the same feature.

**V. What the yardstick leaves out, and why it still holds (≈175)**
- Admit the limits: measured by shared facts or trust, the picture is bleaker, and a reader could say you chose the kindest test.
- Reply: those harms matter to democracy mainly through participation anyway — a public that can't agree on facts can't hear a new voice properly. So the test isn't soft; it's where the other harms land. If the essay's evidence were drawn from election integrity rather than BLM, the verdict would tilt further negative; say that plainly rather than hiding it.

**VI. Conclusion (≈175)**
- Return to Ferguson: ten years on, the demand that went national in a weekend is still argued over, partly won, largely unlegislated, and now contested by movements using the same tools.
- Restate the split verdict without hedging it into mush: good for who gets to speak, unproven for who gets heard.
- Close on what would settle the question: whether any movement born on these platforms manages, over a decade, to build the offline institutions that turn attention into durable power. Until one does, "good or bad" is the wrong tense; the right one is "not yet decided."
