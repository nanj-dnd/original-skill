# Your Resolution Didn't Fail. It Was Built for Someone Else's Week.

For three years running, my New Year's resolution was the same: gym at 6am. And for three years running, it was dead by February. Not because I stopped wanting it. Because the rotation flipped me to nights, and 6am stopped being the start of my day. It became the tail end of a shift, or the hour I was finally crawling into bed.

I used to file that under willpower. I don't anymore.

Look at almost any advice on making resolutions stick. Same time every day. Build a morning routine. All of it assumes your Monday looks like your Thursday, that you wake and sleep at roughly the same hours all week. If you work 9 to 5, fair enough. If you're on rotating 12s, doing nights in a warehouse, or up every few hours with a newborn, that advice was never written for you. When it falls apart, that isn't a character flaw showing. It's a plan built for a different life.

Here's what finally worked for me, and what I'd try if your week won't hold still.

**Tie the habit to an event, not a clock.** My rule now: the gym is the first thing I do after any shift ends, day or night. No clock involved. A shift ends whether it's 7pm or 7am, so the habit has somewhere to land every time. I've kept it up for 14 months, longer than all three of my 6am attempts put together.

**Pick an event that happens on every kind of day.** Look at your day shifts, your nights and your days off, and find the thing that shows up in all three. If your anchor only exists on day shifts, it'll die the first time the schedule flips, same as a clock time.

**Decide the tired version ahead of time.** Some shifts leave you with nothing. Choose now what counts on those days, even ten minutes, so the habit survives the worst week, not only the average one.

**Expect the flip.** Habits rarely die on an ordinary Tuesday. They die the week the schedule changes, so know what your anchor looks like on transition days before you get there.

None of this takes more discipline than a 6am alarm. It takes less, because you stop fighting your own week.

So if your resolution is already gone, or you can feel it going, check whether it was ever built for the life you actually have. Then give it something to hold on to that isn't a time on the clock.

# Person B

# Ruth (task: resolutions)
67, retired accountant, widowed, writes a blog mostly read by other retirees in her town.

What she thinks: resolutions fail because they're about improving yourself on your own, and nobody is waiting for you to show up. The ones that worked in her life were the ones where someone else was counting on her.

Real case: she resolved to walk more for years and never did. Then her neighbour Joan, recently out of hip surgery, needed someone to walk with, and they've walked every weekday at 9 for two years. "I'd never let Joan down. I let myself down all the time."

Also: she thinks a lot of resolution writing is aimed at young people trying to be productive, and retirees are trying to stay connected, not productive.

How she answers: longer, with small anecdotes; polite; sometimes goes off on a tangent.
