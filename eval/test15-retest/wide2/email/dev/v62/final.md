**Angle:** Lead on the hours your dispatchers spend planning routes each morning, not on fuel, and make one honest, attributed claim instead of a list of percentages.
**Built from:** 1c (dispatcher planning time), 2d (customers report about 2 hours down to 20 minutes, and no unsupported savings), 3a (15-minute call), 4b (plain, from a rep, reusable).
**Strongest objection:** A head of ops may see planning time as a small cost next to fuel and drivers, so "customers say" could read as soft. **Test:** Before you send it widely, ask two or three customers whether they'll confirm the two-hours-to-twenty-minutes figure if a prospect asks. Then send this version and a fuel-led version to about 20 contacts each and compare reply rates.
**Other direction:** Swap the call for a free analysis of one week of their route data, so the planning-time claim gets proven on their own operation.

**Subject:** Two hours of route planning, or twenty minutes?

Hi,

In a lot of mid-size logistics operations, the day starts with someone matching the day's orders to drivers and vehicles. It gets done, but it eats a big part of the morning.

Our route-optimisation software is built to cut that work down. Customers tell us their daily dispatch planning has gone from about two hours to around twenty minutes. That's time back for the problems that genuinely need a person.

I won't quote you a fuel or mileage saving before I've seen how your routes run. But if planning time is a cost you feel, would you be open to a 15-minute call to see whether your team could get the same result?

Thanks,

**Before you use it**
- The email ends at "Thanks," so your email signature supplies your name, title and product. If your signature doesn't name the product, add it to the second paragraph ("Our software, [name], is built to...").
- The greeting is "Hi," so it works without a merge field. If your tool personalises, use "Hi {first name},".
- Ask one customer whether you can name them next to the two-hour figure. A named source is the single strongest addition you could make.

**Distance from the default:** The headline moved from fuel and miles to dispatcher planning time (1c). The invented percentages and the feature list are gone, leaving one attributed claim (2d). It now openly declines to quote savings, which none of the samples do. It still shares two things with the samples: a call as the ask, which you chose in 3a, and a general opener about mid-size operations.
