# Why Your New Year's Resolution Probably Won't Make It to March

Every January, the gym is packed. By mid-February, you can have any machine you want. Most of us have been part of that disappearing crowd at some point, and the usual explanation is that we just didn't want it badly enough. I don't buy that. Most resolutions fail for reasons that have very little to do with willpower.

**They're wishes, not plans.** "Get healthy" and "read more" sound like goals, but they don't tell you what to do on a Tuesday night when you're tired. A resolution with no specific action attached has nothing to hold onto when motivation fades. And motivation always fades.

**They're too big, too fast.** January 1st feels like a clean slate, so we try to rebuild our whole lives at once: new diet, daily workouts, no phone after 9pm, journaling every morning. Every change costs energy. Stack five of them and you're running on empty by week two.

**They fight the old routine instead of fitting into it.** Your days are already full of habits, cues, and defaults. If a new behavior has to battle all of them, the defaults usually win. Resolve to cook more but keep driving past the takeout place at 7pm, hungry, and you've set up a fight you'll lose most nights.

**One slip feels like total failure.** This is the big one. You miss a workout, eat the cake, skip a day of writing, and suddenly the whole project feels broken. "Well, I already blew it" has ended more resolutions than any lack of discipline. Missing once is normal. Missing once and deciding you're done is what actually kills the habit.

**The timing is arbitrary.** There's nothing magic about January 1st. It's often a lousy time to start anything: you're worn out from the holidays, the days are short, and everyone around you is starting and quitting on the same schedule.

So what helps? Pick one thing, not five. Make it small enough that you could do it on your worst day. Attach it to something you already do, like ten push-ups right after you brush your teeth. Decide ahead of time what happens when you miss a day (answer: you do it the next day, no drama). And if January isn't the right moment, start in March. Nobody's checking.

The goal isn't to become a new person overnight. It's to become slightly different, consistently, for long enough that it sticks.


# Person A

# Tomás (task: resolutions)
47, former smoker for 15 years, quit on his 4th serious attempt (6 years smoke-free). Works as a high-school chemistry teacher.

What he thinks: "most resolutions fail" is the wrong frame. Most attempts fail, and that's normal; his first three quits weren't failures, they were how he learned what triggered him (the first coffee, driving, his brother). He'd count attempts, not outcomes, and he's read that most successful quitters needed several attempts.

Real case: attempt 2 lasted 5 weeks and broke at a wedding. That's how he learned weddings and drinking were the trigger, and attempt 4 planned around exactly that.

Hates: "be kind to yourself" fluff, and anything that sounds like a self-help book.

Blog readers: friends, some former students, people from a quit-smoking forum.

How he answers: blunt, a teacher's habit of correcting framing.


# Person B

# Ruth (task: resolutions)
67, retired accountant, widowed, writes a blog mostly read by other retirees in her town.

What she thinks: resolutions fail because they're about improving yourself on your own, and nobody is waiting for you to show up. The ones that worked in her life were the ones where someone else was counting on her.

Real case: she resolved to walk more for years and never did. Then her neighbour Joan, recently out of hip surgery, needed someone to walk with, and they've walked every weekday at 9 for two years. "I'd never let Joan down. I let myself down all the time."

Also: she thinks a lot of resolution writing is aimed at young people trying to be productive, and retirees are trying to stay connected, not productive.

How she answers: longer, with small anecdotes; polite; sometimes goes off on a tangent.
