# Original run (quick): why most New Year's resolutions fail

Quick mode: no questions asked, so no pick from the user. Everything below works from the task alone.

## Fixed and open parts

- **Fixed:** about 400 words, a personal blog, the topic, and any statistic used. The usual "80% of resolutions fail by February" figure has no traceable study behind it. The studies that do exist: Norcross, Mrykalo & Blagys (2002, *Journal of Clinical Psychology*) found 46% of resolvers vs 4% of comparable non-resolvers still succeeding at six months. Oscarsson et al. (2020, *PLOS ONE*, ~1,000 participants) found 55% self-reported success at one year, with approach goals at about 59% vs avoidance goals at about 47%. (These were checked from memory only. This run had no web access, so confirm them before publishing.)
- **Open:** the claim, the angle, which reasons to include, the structure, the assumed reader.

## What everyone gets

Main claim: most resolutions fail (around 80% by February) because they are too vague and too ambitious and they rely on willpower, so you should set small, specific goals and build habits.

Choices it made:
- **Thesis:** the failure rate is real and the cause is the goal-setter's technique.
- **Evidence:** the 80% statistic, often with the "21 days to form a habit" myth.
- **Structure:** a shocking stat, then 3 to 5 numbered reasons, then SMART-goal tips, then an upbeat sign-off.
- **Reader:** a generic self-improver who has already failed and needs a pep talk.
- **Leaves out:** whether the "most fail" premise is true, and how "failure" is being counted.

## Candidates

Banned idea: the default's main claim (they fail because they're vague, too big and willpower-based, so go small and specific).

1. The premise is shaky. The 80% figure has no source, and the real studies show resolvers beating non-resolvers by a wide margin. *(the fixed fact sits badly with the default's claim)*
2. Resolutions "fail" because we grade them pass/fail, and early. Partial success gets counted as zero. *(inverted default choice: how failure is defined)*
3. Failure is the point. A resolution is a cheap yearly experiment, and a "failed" one is data.
4. January is the worst month to start anything: dark, broke, right after the holidays. Move your resolution to a better month. *(inverted default choice: timing)*
5. Announcing a resolution gives you some of the identity reward early, so you work less for it (Gollwitzer et al., 2009). *(reader who would disagree: the "tell everyone for accountability" crowd)*
6. Avoidance goals ("stop X") do measurably worse than approach goals ("do Y").
7. **[unlikely]** Most resolutions were never meant to succeed. They're a ritual of hope, like a toast, and judging them on completion misreads the genre.
8. What separates the resolutions that stick is that they're boring maintenance, not transformation.
9. Resolutions fail because the real constraint is the calendar, not willpower: an audit of where your week actually goes. *(constraint: a personal blog, first person)*
10. A resolution is often a disguised complaint about something else (a job, a relationship). Fix the symptom and the cause stays put.
11. **[unlikely]** The "most resolutions fail" story causes some of the failure. Every "Quitter's Day" article gives people permission to stop.
12. Resolutions fail because they're solo. The ones that stick come with shared structure: a running club rather than "run more".
13. A post built as a ledger of the author's own resolutions over the years and what became of each. *(constraint: a personal blog)*
14. Instead of adding a resolution, choose one commitment to drop to make room. The fix is subtraction from your schedule, not more willpower.
15. **[unlikely]** Resolutions don't fail. They get quietly renegotiated, and the renegotiated version is often what you actually wanted.

## Cuts

- **3:** a near-duplicate of 2, and the weaker framing. Its "failure is data" point is a common self-help line.
- **6:** taken alone it is close to the default's "write better goals" advice. It survives as supporting evidence inside 1+2.
- **8:** the default reworded ("start small").
- **9:** depends on the user's actual week, and writing it without that would mean making it up.
- **13:** needs the user's real resolution history, which is not in the task and must not be invented.
- **10:** strong, but it takes a therapy-adjacent register that 400 words can't support responsibly.
- **14:** sits close to 6 and 8 (do less, smaller).
- **1 and 2:** merged. The premise is shaky *because of* how failure is counted, so they're one idea.
- **15:** close to 1+2 (both reframe what counts as failure), so it was dropped for spread in favour of 11.
- **5:** dropped for spread. It is also a single study with replication questions.
- No prior-work search: the value of a blog angle doesn't depend on being new to the world.

## Shortlist

**A. They mostly "fail" in the scoring (1+2).** The 80% figure is unsourced, and resolvers do much better than non-resolvers. What looks like failure is pass/fail grading, which turns a slip into quitting. Avoidance goals fail fastest because they're pass/fail by design.
- **Only yours because:** nothing; this is available to anyone. It comes from where the fixed facts contradict the default's premise.
- **Strongest objection:** both studies rely on self-reported success, so "46% succeeding" may be generous.
- **Test:** the Norcross 46%/4% and Oscarsson approach/avoidance figures must hold up when checked against the papers.

**B. Don't start in January (4).** Resolutions fail partly because of when they start: the darkest, most broke, most over-scheduled weeks of the year. Pick a "new year" that fits your life.
- **Only yours because:** nothing; this is available to anyone.
- **Strongest objection:** the "fresh start effect" research (Dai, Milkman & Riis) suggests temporal landmarks help motivation, and January 1 is the biggest one.
- **Test:** whether people who start a goal in a non-January month keep it at a higher rate. That is checkable but unknown here.

**C. Solo resolutions fail; shared ones stick (12).** The individual resolution is the wrong unit. What keeps people going is a structure other people depend on.
- **Only yours because:** nothing; this is available to anyone.
- **Strongest objection:** plenty of people keep solo goals, and group commitments fail when the group dissolves.
- **Test:** it would be undercut by evidence that social-support resolvers do no better. Norcross (2002) found social support mattered little early on and more later.

**D. [unlikely] The "most fail" story is self-fulfilling (11).** Annual "you've probably already quit" content gives people permission and a timetable to quit.
- **Only yours because:** nothing; this is available to anyone.
- **Strongest objection:** it is hard to show that media framing changes individual behaviour, and it may just be blaming the messenger.
- **Test:** a prediction that quit rates rise around "Quitter's Day" coverage more than activity data alone would explain.

## Developing

**A.** It has the most tension with the default, because it uses the task's own fixed facts against the task's premise. It still answers the user's question (why do most *seem* to fail?) and fits 400 words. B and C are good posts but sit closer to standard advice. D is the boldest of the four, but its central claim is the hardest to support.
