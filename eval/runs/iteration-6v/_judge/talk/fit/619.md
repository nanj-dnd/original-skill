# Output 619

# Not Everything, But More Than You Think
### A 5-minute all-hands talk on AI coding assistants

**Thesis:** "Should we use AI for everything?" is the wrong question. The real skill is knowing which work to hand off and which to keep our hands on. And you own every line you ship, whoever typed it.

---

## Outline (about 5:00 total, roughly 650–700 spoken words)

**1. Opening: reframe the question (0:00–0:45)**
- Show of hands, then reveal the gap (see the opening paragraph below).
- The real question isn't yes or no. It's *where*.

**2. Where it clearly earns its keep (0:45–1:45)**
- Boilerplate, scaffolding, config, glue code.
- Writing tests, and finding edge cases you hadn't thought of.
- Getting up to speed on an unfamiliar library, API, or legacy file.
- Mechanical refactors and migrations.
- First drafts of docs, scripts, and one-off tooling.
- *The common thread:* the output is cheap to check. If it's wrong, you'll know fast.
- Not using it here leaves real time on the table.

**3. Where "everything" breaks down (1:45–3:00)**
- Code you can't easily verify: security, auth, concurrency, money, data migrations, core domain logic.
- Confidently wrong answers. It looks right, reads right, and passes a quick glance.
- The review burden moves. If the author didn't really understand it, the reviewer now has to.
- Skill atrophy, especially early in a career. You can't judge output you couldn't have written yourself.
- *The common thread:* being wrong is expensive and checking is hard.

**4. A simple rule of thumb: "Delegate the typing, not the thinking" (3:00–4:00)**
Before you merge AI-assisted code, ask three questions:
1. **Can I verify it?** With tests, by reading it, or by running it.
2. **Can I explain it?** Line by line, to my reviewer.
3. **Can I fix it at 2 a.m.?** If it breaks in production, am I lost?
- Three yeses: go fast and use it freely. Any no: slow down or write it yourself.

**5. What this means for us (4:00–4:40)**
*Pick two or three that fit our team and current policy:*
- Same review bar for all code, no matter who or what wrote it.
- Share what works: prompts, workflows, and failure stories in [team channel].
- Mention significant AI assistance in the PR description so reviewers know where to look harder.
- Newer engineers: write the hard parts by hand sometimes. That's how judgment gets built.

**6. Close (4:40–5:00)**
- Call back to the opening: the goal is that every hand that goes up for "I used AI this week" stays up for "and I can explain every line."
- Closing line: *"Use it for more than you think. Just never for the part where you stop thinking."*

---

## Opening paragraph (about 45 seconds)

> Quick show of hands: who used an AI assistant to write some code this week? *[pause, let hands go up]* Now keep your hand up if you'd be comfortable explaining every line it wrote to the person reviewing your PR. *[pause]* That gap, between the hands that went up and the hands that stayed up, is what I want to talk about for the next five minutes. "Should engineers use AI for everything?" sounds like a yes-or-no question, but it isn't. These tools are genuinely good at a lot of our work, and refusing to use them leaves real time on the table. But "everything" is the wrong goal. The right goal is knowing which work to hand off and which to keep our hands on, and owning every line we ship either way.
