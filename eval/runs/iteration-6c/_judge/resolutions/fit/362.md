# Count the attempts

Every January someone repeats the line that most New Year's resolutions are dead by February. That number gets passed around far more often than it gets checked. But suppose it's right. It still measures the wrong thing. It looks at whether your first attempt is still running on one particular day, and treats the answer as a verdict on you.

That isn't how anything hard gets done. I smoked for fifteen years. I quit for good on my fourth serious try, and I've been clean for six years now. By the February test, I failed three times. I don't count it that way.

My second attempt lasted five weeks and ended at a wedding. Before that night I thought my problem was willpower. After it I knew something more useful: weddings plus drinking would get me. The other attempts filled in the rest of the list: the first coffee of the day, driving, my brother. None of those attempts was a failure in any sense that matters. Each one was the only way I had of finding out what I was up against.

Attempt four wasn't braver than the others. It was better informed. I planned it around exactly what the first three had shown me. What changed wasn't willpower. I knew what would get me.

This isn't just my story. A long-running Canadian study of smokers (Chaiton and colleagues, *BMJ Open*, 2016) estimated that people who finally quit had made somewhere between six and thirty attempts first, depending on how you count. Ex-smokers aren't the people who never slipped. Most of them slipped a lot and went again.

So "most resolutions fail" mixes up attempts and outcomes. Most attempts fail. That's normal, and on its own it decides very little. What decides whether you get there is whether you make another attempt, and whether it's different from the last one.

That second part matters, and it's where the comfortable version of this argument goes soft. A failed attempt is only data if you read it. If your fourth try is a copy of your first, with the same plan and the same wedding and the same drinks, you haven't run four experiments. You've run one experiment four times.

If you're reading this after a relapse, and I know some of you are, the useful question isn't whether you failed. You did. So did I, three times. The question is what got you, and whether your next attempt is built around it.

# Person B

# Ruth (task: resolutions)
67, retired accountant, widowed, writes a blog mostly read by other retirees in her town.

What she thinks: resolutions fail because they're about improving yourself on your own, and nobody is waiting for you to show up. The ones that worked in her life were the ones where someone else was counting on her.

Real case: she resolved to walk more for years and never did. Then her neighbour Joan, recently out of hip surgery, needed someone to walk with, and they've walked every weekday at 9 for two years. "I'd never let Joan down. I let myself down all the time."

Also: she thinks a lot of resolution writing is aimed at young people trying to be productive, and retirees are trying to stay connected, not productive.

How she answers: longer, with small anecdotes; polite; sometimes goes off on a tangent.
