# Why most New Year's resolutions fail: quick run

Mode: quick. No questions were asked; this works from the task alone.

## Fixed and open parts

- **Fixed:** any statistics or studies cited, and the ~400 words for a personal blog.
  - The common "80% fail by February" figure has no traceable source, so it is not used.
  - Norcross & Vangarelli (1988–89) found that 77% of resolvers kept their resolution for 1 week, 55% for 1 month, 40% for 6 months and 19% for 2 years.
  - Norcross, Mrykalo & Blagys (2002, *J. Clin. Psychol.*) found 46% of resolvers succeeding at 6 months, against 4% of non-resolvers who had similar goals.
  - Strava named a mid-January "Quitter's Day" from its users' activity data.
- **Open:** the claim, the angle, the examples, the structure and the assumed reader.

## What everyone gets

Main claim: resolutions fail because they're vague or too ambitious and depend on willpower and motivation, which fade. The fix is to set small, specific goals and build habits and systems.

The choices that default makes:
- **Thesis:** the failure is personal. It comes from bad goal design plus weak willpower.
- **Hook:** the "80% fail by February" stat, given without a source.
- **Structure:** a listicle of 3–5 reasons, then matching tips (SMART goals, start small, track progress, habit stacking, be kind to yourself).
- **Reader:** a generic self-improvement reader who wants to be told what to do.
- **Leaves out:** that resolving beats not resolving, and the fact that everyone resolves at the same moment.

## Candidates

Banned: the default's main claim, that resolutions fail because they're vague or ambitious and rely on willpower instead of systems.

1. "Most fail" is the wrong comparison. Resolvers succeed at over ten times the rate of people with the same goals who don't resolve. *(start: a fixed fact that sits badly with the default)*
2. The "80% by February" stat is folklore. The real data is a decay curve that looks like any behaviour change, with nothing special about January. *(start: fixed fact)*
3. They fail because we score them pass/fail, so one lapse reads as "broken" and we quit. *(start: inverted default choice, the reasons-then-tips structure)*
4. January 1 is a bad start date: dark, broke, post-holiday. The fresh-start effect is real, but you can pick a better date. *(start: inverted default choice)*
5. Most resolutions were never yours. They come from what January sells (gym, diet, dry month), so dropping them is honest. *(start: the reader who would disagree)*
6. "Stop X" goals fail more than "start Y" goals, so the wording matters more than the willpower. *(start: inverted default choice)*
7. A resolution's job isn't to last the year. A two-week resolution that taught you something has done its job. *(start: the reader who would disagree)*
8. A confessional post about the resolution I've made nine years running. *(start: the personal-blog constraint)*
9. Announcing a resolution gives you the identity reward early, so telling people makes you less likely to do it. *(start: the reader who would disagree)*
10. Resolutions are a group ritual that we mistake for a private decision. They fail when the season ends and the crowd leaves, taking the borrowed motivation with it. *(start: the fact everyone resolves at once, which the default leaves out)*
11. The year is the wrong unit. Nobody plans anything else on a 12-month horizon without checkpoints. *(start: inverted default choice)*
12. Failing resolutions go unnoticed as a success story: plenty of people change things quietly mid-year, and those changes never get counted. *(start: fixed fact)*
13. **[unlikely]** A failed resolution is useful data. It measures the gap between who you think you should be and who you are, and that gap is the honest finding.
14. **[unlikely]** Resign instead of resolve: make the resolution a subtraction, quitting an obligation instead of adding a habit.
15. **[unlikely]** Start in December, so that Jan 1 is a checkpoint and not a starting line.

## Cuts

- **2:** Folded into the facts. It works as a correction inside another post but is thin as a thesis on its own.
- **4:** Close to 15. Of the two, 15 keeps the stronger version.
- **6:** Mostly the default reworded (goal-design advice), and the effect in Oscarsson et al. 2020 is modest.
- **8:** Needs the user's own history. In quick mode with no answers, it would mean inventing a personal anecdote under their name.
- **9:** Rests on Gollwitzer 2009, which has had replication doubts. A fixed fact is too weak to carry it.
- **11:** The default reworded ("time-bound" is the T in SMART).
- **12:** A near-duplicate of 1.
- **14:** A fixed fact works against it, because avoidance-framed goals did worse in Oscarsson 2020.
- **15:** A near-duplicate of 4 and a tip, not an answer to "why".
- **3:** Strong, but close to well-worn advice ("never miss twice", abstinence-violation effect). It lost on spread to 7.

## Shortlist

**A. The crowd leaves (10).** A resolution is the one personal goal we set at the same moment as everyone else. Much of its early motivation is borrowed from that collective season, so it collapses when the season ends in mid-January.
- **Only yours because:** nothing; this is available to anyone. It was picked because it is furthest from the default's "it's your willpower" framing.
- **Strongest objection:** any behaviour change decays fastest in the first weeks, so the January drop may just be the normal curve with a crowd around it.
- **Test:** a prediction. Resolutions started on a private fresh-start date, such as a birthday, should show a smaller drop in weeks 2–4 than January ones. The Norcross 1988 curve (77% at 1 week, 55% at 1 month) has to fit the claim, and it does.

**B. Compared to what? (1).** Most resolutions do fail, but people who resolve succeed at over ten times the rate of people who want the same change and don't. The real story is that resolving works surprisingly well.
- **Only yours because:** nothing; this is available to anyone.
- **Strongest objection:** the resolvers chose to resolve, so they may simply have been more ready to change (self-selection).
- **Test:** Norcross et al. 2002, 46% vs 4% at six months, with non-resolvers who had comparable goals.

**C. A resolution isn't meant to last a year (7).** We call a resolution failed if it doesn't survive twelve months, a standard we apply to no other experiment. Two weeks that taught you something is a result.
- **Only yours because:** nothing; this is available to anyone.
- **Strongest objection:** it can read as a rationalisation for quitting.
- **Test:** it has to survive the rebuttal "then why make it a year-long promise?" The post would need an answer, for example that the year-long frame is the mistake.

**D. It was never your resolution (5).** January hands everyone the same menu of resolutions (gym, diet, dry month, read more). Many fail because they were picked off that menu, not out of anything the person wanted.
- **Only yours because:** nothing; this is available to anyone.
- **Strongest objection:** "lose weight" can be both culturally sold and something you sincerely want.
- **Test:** a prediction. The most common resolutions should also be the ones dropped fastest, and idiosyncratic ones should last longer.

**E. Failure as a measurement (13, unlikely).** A dropped resolution measures the gap between who you think you should be and who you are. That gap is worth reading before you set the next one.
- **Only yours because:** nothing; this is available to anyone.
- **Strongest objection:** it is abstract and could slide into self-help mush in 400 words.
- **Test:** it has to survive the rebuttal "sometimes you just got busy". Not every lapse says something about you.

## Picked

**A, the crowd leaves**, using the fact from B as support.

It is the furthest of the five from the default. The default says the failure is in you; A says it is mostly in your surroundings, and that changes what the advice should be. Its facts are checkable (Norcross 1988 and 2002, Strava's Quitter's Day). It also fits a personal blog: it is one argument with a clear turn, not a listicle.
