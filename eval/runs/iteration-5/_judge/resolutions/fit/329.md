# Why Most New Year's Resolutions Fail (And It's Not Because You're Lazy)

Every January I watch the same thing happen. The gym fills up and everyone has a new journal. By mid-February the gym is back to normal and the journals are in a drawer. Mine included.

For years I treated that as a willpower problem, as if I just hadn't wanted it badly enough. I don't think that's true anymore. I think most resolutions fail because of how they're built, not because of the people making them.

## The goal is a wish, not a plan

"Get healthy." "Read more." "Be less stressed." These sound like goals, but they're really wishes. They don't tell you what to do on a Tuesday at 6 p.m. when you're tired, so you end up running on mood, and mood doesn't last.

Compare "read more" with "read ten pages before I look at my phone in the morning." The second one is smaller and less inspiring, and it's much more likely to happen.

## We try to change everything at once

January 1st feels like a clean slate, so we load it up: new diet, new workout routine, no alcohol, meditate daily, learn Spanish. Each habit draws on the same limited energy. Stack five on top of a normal life and usually it all goes.

## One bad day becomes the whole year

This is the big one for me. I miss a workout, decide the streak is broken, and figure I might as well stop. Psychologists sometimes call this the "what-the-hell effect." A resolution set up as a perfect daily streak is brittle, because a single slip feels like failing the whole thing.

Real progress is messy. Missing one day hardly matters. Missing one day and then deciding you've failed is what actually ends it.

## We focus on outcomes instead of identity

"Lose 20 pounds" is a finish line far off in the distance. "Be someone who goes for a walk after dinner" is something you can be tonight. When the habit is tied to who you are, each small action counts as a win on its own.

## So what actually works?

Pick one thing. Make it small enough that it feels a little silly. Attach it to something you already do. Plan for the days you'll miss, because you will. And remember you don't need January to start. Any random Wednesday works just as well.

Maybe that's the real resolution: be a bit more patient with yourself.

What he thinks: "most resolutions fail" is the wrong frame. Hates: "be kind to yourself" fluff, and anything that sounds like a self-help book.

# Person B

# Leah (task: resolutions)
ER nurse, 34, works rotating 12-hour shifts (days, then nights, changing every few weeks).

What she thinks: resolution advice assumes you have a regular schedule. "Same time every day" and "morning routine" are impossible for shift workers, nurses, warehouse staff, parents of newborns. Resolutions fail for a lot of people because the advice is written for 9-to-5 office workers.

Real case: tried a 6am gym habit three years running and failed every time by February because of rotation. What finally worked: anchoring it to an event, not a time: "the gym is the first thing after any shift ends, day or night." She's kept it 14 months.

Blog readers: mostly other nurses and healthcare workers, from her Instagram.

She dislikes generic advice and the word "simply".

How she answers: practical, warm, a bit wry; gives specifics.
