# Why Most New Year's Resolutions Fail

Every year, as December comes to an end, we find ourselves making promises to a future version of ourselves. We'll go to the gym, read more books, spend less time on our phones, eat healthier, or finally learn that skill we've been putting off. For a few days, sometimes even a few weeks, we're genuinely committed. And then, slowly, things go back to normal.

So, why does this happen?

I think the biggest reason most New Year's resolutions fail is that we rely too heavily on motivation.

There's something about the beginning of a new year that makes change feel exciting. A fresh calendar, a clean slate, and the feeling that this time things will be different. Motivation is at an all-time high, and suddenly, waking up at 5 AM or going for a run every morning doesn't seem so difficult.

But motivation is an emotion, and like most emotions, it comes and goes.

Eventually, the excitement wears off. You have a long day, it's raining outside, you don't feel like going to the gym, or you simply have other things on your mind. And that's when the real test begins. Unfortunately, most resolutions aren't built to survive those moments.

We often mistake wanting something badly for being prepared to work towards it consistently. The two are very different.

The problem isn't necessarily that we set ambitious goals. It's that we build our plans around the assumption that we'll feel motivated every single day. We forget that progress is rarely exciting. More often than not, it's repetitive, inconvenient, and sometimes downright boring.

I think the solution is to stop treating resolutions as temporary bursts of enthusiasm and start treating them as habits.

Instead of promising to read 30 books in a year, start by reading five pages every day. Instead of committing to an intense workout routine, start with something you can realistically maintain. Make the barrier to starting as small as possible.

And perhaps most importantly, learn to show up even when you don't feel like it.

Missing a day shouldn't mean abandoning the entire goal. One bad week shouldn't become an excuse to give up for the rest of the year.

Ultimately, I don't think the people who achieve their New Year's resolutions are necessarily more motivated than everyone else. They're simply better at continuing when motivation disappears.

Maybe that's what we should focus on this January: not becoming a completely different person overnight, but becoming someone who keeps showing up, even on ordinary days.
