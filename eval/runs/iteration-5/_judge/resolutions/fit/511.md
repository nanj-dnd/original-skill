# I Kept 2 of My Last 11 New Year's Resolutions. The 2 Had One Thing in Common.

For three years I've tracked my New Year's resolutions in a spreadsheet. Eleven resolutions in total. Two stuck.

That's about 18%, close to what researchers found in 1989, when psychologists John Norcross and Dominic Vangarelli followed 200 resolvers: 77% were still going after a week, and only 19% after two years. So I'm not unusually bad at this. Most of us aren't.

The usual explanation is that resolutions are too vague or too big. But in my spreadsheet, what split winners from losers was how often the resolution asked me to decide.

Every resolution that failed needed a decision every day. Each day was a fresh vote, and losing a few was enough to kill it. A resolution like that doesn't fail once. It gets 365 chances to fail.

The two that stuck needed one decision, made once.

**Savings.** I set up an automatic transfer to savings on payday. The money moves whether I feel motivated or not.

**Fitness.** I cancelled my gym membership and bought a doorway pull-up bar. One setup action, and from then on it ran on its own. It's not as hands-off as the transfer, since I still have to do the pull-ups, but there's no trip to talk myself out of and no membership I'm paying for and not using.

Personal finance figured this out a long time ago. When one large US company switched its 401(k) plan from opt-in to automatic enrollment, participation among new hires jumped from about 37% to 86% (Madrian and Shea, 2001). Same plan. The only change was who had to make the decision.

Willpower doesn't scale. Automation does.

So before you write this year's list, put each resolution through one question: **can I turn this into a single setup action?**

- "Save more" becomes a transfer scheduled for payday.
- "Invest regularly" becomes a recurring contribution to your brokerage or retirement account.
- "Pay down the card" becomes autopay set above the minimum.
- "Spend less on subscriptions" becomes cancelling them today.
- "Work out" becomes removing the friction: equipment at home, and cancelling what you're paying for but not using.

If a resolution can't be set up once, shrink it until it can, or accept that it will be a daily vote, and daily votes are the ones most of us lose.

Eleven tries taught me one rule: if you can't set it up once, don't count on keeping it.
