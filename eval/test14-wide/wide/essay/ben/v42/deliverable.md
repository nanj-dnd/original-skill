**Angle**: Social media's democratic goods come from the open network and its democratic harms come from engagement ranking; because they are produced by different layers, they don't net out into a balance, and the verdict is "bad for now, for a reason that is specific and still changeable."

**Strongest objection**: The best field evidence cuts against the ranking story. # Thesis

Social media has been bad for democracy so far, but not in the way the question assumes. A platform is two things laid on top of each other: an open network that lets anyone publish, organise, and bear witness without permission, and a ranking system that decides which of those posts reach whom. The democratic gains of the past fifteen years, from the Arab Spring to #MeToo, come from the first layer; the democratic harms, from the amplification of ethnic hatred in Myanmar to the industrial-scale manipulation of the 2016 US election, track the second, where what spreads is whatever keeps people engaged, and what keeps people engaged is outrage at each other. Because the good and the harm are generated by different parts of the system, weighing them as though they sit on one scale is the wrong exercise. The honest verdict is that social media as actually built has damaged the conditions democracy depends on, and that the damage is located in a business decision rather than in the technology, which is why the verdict is conditional rather than final.

# Outline (1,500 words)

## I. Introduction (200 words)
- Open analytically, not with a contrast: state that a social media platform is a network and a ranking, and that "good or bad for democracy" has been answered confidently in both directions because each side is looking at a different layer.
- Note that the same tools were called "liberation technology" in 2011 and a threat to democracy by 2021, and that the essay's task is to explain the reversal rather than pick a decade.
- State that the essay will set a criterion for "good for democracy," sort the familiar cases against it, and let the verdict follow.
- Thesis, in one to two sentences.

## II. What would count as good or bad (200 words)
- Define what a functioning democracy needs: citizens who can speak and organise; a public that broadly agrees on what happened, even while disagreeing about what to do; and losers who accept results because they regard winners as opponents rather than enemies.
- Explain why this criterion matters: it stops the essay from counting "more people can post" as automatically good or "more falsehood exists" as automatically bad, and asks instead whether each outcome strengthens or weakens one of those three conditions.
- Signal the method: the next two sections test the open network and the ranking system separately against this criterion.

## III. The open-network layer, tested (300 words)
- Claim: judged against the criterion, the network itself has been good for the first condition, voice and organisation, and has not obviously harmed the others.
- Arab Spring (2010 to 2011): coordination of protest at a cost no state could police in advance; the gain was speed of assembly, not persuasion.
- #MeToo (2017) and phone-camera documentation of police violence: witness that legacy editors had declined to run reached the public anyway. This is the gatekeeping argument, used only as far as it holds: the goods here are goods of access, not of accuracy.
- Concede the limit honestly: these movements show what a network makes possible, and none of them required a feed that ranks by engagement. Transition: the question is what happens when ranking is added.

## IV. The ranking layer, tested (350 words)
- Claim: the harms attach to the decision to distribute content by predicted engagement, which selects for anger and in-group loyalty because those are what hold attention.
- Mechanism in plain terms: ad-funded platforms earn more the longer users stay; the ranking system is tuned to that goal; content that provokes moral outrage reliably wins the tuning. The user does nothing wrong by reacting; the incentive is upstream of the user.
- Evidence to cite by name: Vosoughi, Roy and Aral (*Science*, 2018) on false news spreading further and faster than true news on Twitter; the 2021 Facebook internal documents disclosed by Frances Haugen showing the company knew its ranking changes favoured divisive content.
- Cases: Myanmar, where the UN fact-finding mission in 2018 found Facebook had played a significant role in spreading hatred against the Rohingya; the 2016 US election, where Russia's Internet Research Agency did not hack the network but exploited the ranking, buying reach for content designed to inflame.
- Tie each back to the criterion: both cases damage the second and third conditions, shared facts and the legitimacy of opponents, which the network cases in Section III never touched.

## V. The test the thesis has to pass (250 words)
- State the strongest counter-evidence fairly: Boxell, Gentzkow and Shapiro (2017) found US polarisation rose fastest among the demographic least on social media; Guess et al. (*Science*, 2023) found that switching users to chronological feeds for three months reduced time on platform but did not measurably reduce polarisation.
- Work it out rather than wave it off. Two readings are available:
  - The ranking story is wrong, and the harm comes from television, partisan sorting, or economics, with social media as bystander.
  - The ranking story is incomplete: three months on a chronological feed inside an ecosystem already shaped by a decade of ranked feeds is not the same as a world that never had them, and the Myanmar and 2016 cases involve scale and speed of spread that chronological ordering alone does not remove.
- Commit, measuredly: the evidence supports the second reading, but it narrows the claim. The harm lives in engagement ranking operating at scale and speed, not in ranking alone. This is where the essay "works it out" and where the thesis earns its conditional wording.

## VI. Conclusion (200 words)
- Return to the two layers. The goods and the harms were never on one scale; the network produced the former and the ranking the latter.
- Deliver the verdict in its earned form: bad for democracy as built, because the version that was built chose engagement as its organising incentive; not bad in a way that the technology makes permanent.
- Close on what the conditional verdict implies without turning into a policy essay: the question that remains open is whether distribution can be run on a different incentive, and that question is a matter of design and law rather than of citizens behaving better.
