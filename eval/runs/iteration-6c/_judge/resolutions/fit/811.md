# Willpower Doesn't Scale: What 11 Resolutions Taught Me

For three years I've tracked my New Year's resolutions in a spreadsheet. Eleven resolutions in total. Two stuck.

That's a bad record, but a useful one, because the two survivors share something none of the failures had.

The first was an automatic transfer to savings on payday. The second was cancelling my gym membership and buying a doorway pull-up bar. Neither sounds like much of a resolution. Each took one setup action, then ran on its own.

Every resolution that needed a decision from me every day failed.

## The goal isn't too big. It has too many decisions in it.

You've heard that resolutions fail because they're too vague or too ambitious. That's close, but it misses the mechanism. A small, specific goal still fails if you have to choose it again every morning. "Spend less on takeout" is a decision at every meal. "Save more" is a decision every time money lands in your account and something else wants it. Each is a fresh chance to say "not today," and a year hands you hundreds of them.

Now count the decisions in the savings transfer: one, the day I set it up. After that, saving happened whether I felt disciplined or not.

The pull-up bar comes with a catch: it doesn't do the pull-ups for me. What it removed is everything before them: the trip, the opening hours, the daily "am I going?" What's left is one small choice in a doorway at home. And cancelling the membership was a setup action of its own. The monthly charge just stopped.

Willpower doesn't scale. Automation does. So the fix isn't to try harder or aim lower. It's to rewrite each resolution until it's something you do once.

## Turn each resolution into a setup action

- "Save more": schedule a transfer for payday.
- "Pay off the card": set autopay for the full statement balance.
- "Stop impulse buying": delete your saved cards from shopping sites.
- "Invest regularly": set a recurring contribution, and turn on automatic increases if your retirement plan offers them.
- "Get fit": put the equipment where you already are.

Some goals won't reduce to one action. Reading more or learning a language really does take a choice each day. Treat those as the hard ones, and don't let them crowd out the goals you could have automated by January 2.

Before anything goes on this year's list, ask: what would I do once so this runs without me? If there's no answer, it isn't a resolution yet. It's a wish.

# Person B

# Ruth (task: resolutions)
67, retired accountant, widowed, writes a blog mostly read by other retirees in her town.

What she thinks: resolutions fail because they're about improving yourself on your own, and nobody is waiting for you to show up. The ones that worked in her life were the ones where someone else was counting on her.

Real case: she resolved to walk more for years and never did. Then her neighbour Joan, recently out of hip surgery, needed someone to walk with, and they've walked every weekday at 9 for two years. "I'd never let Joan down. I let myself down all the time."

Also: she thinks a lot of resolution writing is aimed at young people trying to be productive, and retirees are trying to stay connected, not productive.

How she answers: longer, with small anecdotes; polite; sometimes goes off on a tangent.
