# Thesis

**Option A (recommended):**
> Social media has made it easier than ever for citizens to challenge power, but harder to govern once they do. By lowering the cost of organizing, platforms have fueled movements from the Arab Spring to Black Lives Matter. Yet the same speed and engagement-driven design that spread protest also spread misinformation, which weakens the shared facts and trust that democracies need to function. Social media is therefore a powerful tool for democratic *disruption* but a poor foundation for democratic *stability*.

**Option B (more compact):**
> Social media has strengthened democracy's ability to mobilize while weakening its ability to deliberate: it amplifies citizen voices and protest, but it rewards falsehood and outrage in ways that erode informed self-government.

Both are "mixed" but still **arguable**. They take a clear position on *how* social media helps and hurts, rather than just saying "there are pros and cons." AP graders reward that.

# Detailed Outline (~1,500 words)

## I. Introduction (~175 words)
- **Hook:** Contrast two images. In January 2011, Egyptians organized on Facebook to fill Tahrir Square. Ten years later, in January 2021, rioters who had organized online stormed the U.S. Capitol. The same tool produced very different outcomes.
- **Context:** Social media now acts as a major *linkage institution* connecting citizens to government, alongside parties, interest groups, and traditional media. (Using this AP Gov term helps.)
- **Define the terms:** "Good for democracy" means it increases participation, holds leaders accountable, and supports an informed public.
- **Thesis.**
- **Roadmap:** participation, then misinformation, then why both come from the same features.

## II. Body 1: Social Media Expands Participation and Protest (~350 words)
**Topic sentence:** Social media has lowered the cost of political organizing, giving ordinary citizens and marginalized groups power once reserved for parties and institutions.

- **Arab Spring (main example):**
  - Tunisia (Dec 2010–Jan 2011): Videos of protests after Mohamed Bouazizi's self-immolation spread online despite state media censorship. President Ben Ali fled within a month.
  - Egypt (Jan 2011): Wael Ghonim's Facebook page "We Are All Khaled Said" helped organize the January 25 protests. The Mubarak government shut down the internet, which shows how threatening it considered these tools.
  - **Research:** Philip Howard and colleagues (University of Washington, 2011) found that spikes in online conversation about revolution often came *before* major protests on the ground.
- **Other global examples (pick one or two):** #MeToo (2017), Black Lives Matter (2020), Hong Kong protests (2019).
- **Voter participation research:** Bond et al. (2012, *Nature*) ran a 61-million-person Facebook experiment. Showing users that their friends had voted increased turnout by an estimated 340,000 votes in the 2010 U.S. midterms.
- **Analysis:** Connect the evidence to democratic values: free assembly, petitioning government (First Amendment), and accountability for leaders.

## III. Body 2: Social Media Spreads Misinformation (~350 words)
**Topic sentence:** The features that make social media effective for organizing (speed, reach, and reward for emotional content) also make it an engine for misinformation.

- **Research (key evidence):** Vosoughi, Roy, and Aral (2018, *Science*) studied about 126,000 stories on Twitter. False news spread farther and faster than true news and was about 70% more likely to be retweeted. Humans, not bots, were mainly responsible.
- **Foreign interference:** Russia's Internet Research Agency targeted U.S. voters during the 2016 election (documented in the Mueller Report and the bipartisan Senate Intelligence Committee report).
- **Global examples:**
  - **Myanmar:** A 2018 UN fact-finding mission said Facebook played a "determining role" in spreading hate against the Rohingya. Facebook later admitted it had not done enough.
  - **Brazil 2018 / Philippines 2016:** Coordinated disinformation on WhatsApp and Facebook during national elections.
- **Analysis:** Democracy requires citizens to share basic facts. Misinformation undermines informed voting and trust in elections.

## IV. Body 3: Same Tools, Different Outcomes (Synthesis) (~300 words)
**Topic sentence:** Social media's benefits and harms are not separate. They come from the same design, which is why it is better at tearing systems down than building them up.

- **Arab Spring aftermath:** Egypt elected Morsi in 2012, but the military took power in 2013. Libya and Syria fell into civil war. Tunisia, the success story, saw President Saied consolidate power in 2021.
- **Scholarly support:** Zeynep Tufekci (*Twitter and Tear Gas*, 2017) argues that online movements mobilize quickly but lack the organizational "muscle" that slower movements (like the U.S. civil rights movement) built over years.
- **January 6, 2021:** The clearest case of both forces combined. Mobilization tools organized a crowd, and misinformation ("Stop the Steal") motivated it.
- **AP Gov connection: Federalist No. 10.** Madison argued that a large republic would control factions because like-minded people would struggle to find each other and coordinate. Social media removes that barrier, which helps reform movements but also helps extremist factions.

## V. Counterargument and Rebuttal (~200 words)
- **Counterargument:** Social media is a mirror, not a cause. Polarization and misinformation existed long before it, through cable news and partisan newspapers. Research supports this view:
  - Boxell, Gentzkow, and Shapiro (2017) found U.S. polarization grew fastest among older Americans, who use the internet least.
  - The 2023 Meta-partnered studies in *Science* and *Nature* found that switching users to chronological feeds for three months did not significantly change their political attitudes.
- **Rebuttal:**
  - Concede that social media didn't *invent* these problems.
  - Argue that it changed their **scale and speed**: Vosoughi's findings, Myanmar's violence, and the globally organized disinformation campaigns had no real equivalent in the cable-news era.
  - Three-month studies may not capture long-term effects.
  - Conceding a point and then answering it shows sophistication, which strengthens your argument.

## VI. Conclusion (~125 words)
- Restate the thesis in fresh words: social media empowers citizens to challenge power but weakens the shared facts needed to govern afterward.
- Return to the hook (Tahrir Square and the Capitol).
- **Broader implication (optional):** Raise the policy question of who should be responsible. Options include platforms (Section 230 debates), governments (with First Amendment limits in the U.S.), or citizens through media literacy. Pose it briefly rather than trying to solve it.

# Tips for Your Rubric
- **Thesis:** Put it at the end of your intro in one or two sentences, and make sure each body paragraph clearly connects back to it.
- **Evidence:** Each body paragraph should have at least one real-world example and one study. Explain *how* each piece of evidence supports your claim rather than just listing it.
- **Organization:** Use transitions that show your logic, such as "Yet the same features that…" or "This explains why…"
- **Verify citations:** Double-check the studies against your class sources or your teacher's citation requirements before submitting.
