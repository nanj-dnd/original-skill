# Joan Is Waiting on the Step

Every January for years, I resolved to walk more. And every January, give or take a week or two, I stopped. I'm not sure I ever saw it through to February.

You'll have read the usual explanations for this sort of thing. The resolution was too vague, or too big, or you relied too much on willpower. Make it specific, they say. Make it smaller. Write it down.

I'm not so sure that's it. I could have promised myself "twenty minutes a day, round the block and back" and it would have gone exactly the same way. When I skipped it on a wet Tuesday, who noticed? Only me. And if there's one thing most of us have got very good at by now, it's forgiving ourselves. I let myself off all the time. I'm very understanding that way.

Then, two years ago, my neighbour Joan had her hip done. She needed someone to walk with while she got her strength back, so I said I'd go along.

We've walked every weekday at nine o'clock since. Rain, cold, it doesn't matter. I didn't buy new shoes or start keeping a chart. The only thing that changed was that Joan was waiting on her front step. I'd let myself down without a second thought, but I'd never let Joan down. (She's quicker than me now, which I think is a bit cheeky, but there we are.)

I've noticed that most of what's written about resolutions seems to be aimed at younger people trying to get more done. Fair enough, but it doesn't fit many of us. At our age we're not usually trying to be more productive. We're trying to stay connected: to keep seeing people, to have somewhere to be, to be of some use to somebody. And it turns out that's the very thing that makes a resolution stick. Nobody is waiting for you to improve yourself. Somebody might well be waiting for you to turn up.

So I won't give you a list of tips. There are plenty of those about already. I'll just say this. If there's something you'd like to do more of next year, don't go looking for a better system. Look for a person. Someone who'd be glad of the company, or who'd notice if you didn't come. You'll keep it up for them long after you'd have given up on it for yourself.

And if they end up quicker than you, well. You can't have everything.


# Person A

# Tomás (task: resolutions)
47, former smoker for 15 years, quit on his 4th serious attempt (6 years smoke-free). Works as a high-school chemistry teacher.

What he thinks: "most resolutions fail" is the wrong frame. Most attempts fail, and that's normal; his first three quits weren't failures, they were how he learned what triggered him (the first coffee, driving, his brother). He'd count attempts, not outcomes, and he's read that most successful quitters needed several attempts.

Real case: attempt 2 lasted 5 weeks and broke at a wedding. That's how he learned weddings and drinking were the trigger, and attempt 4 planned around exactly that.

Hates: "be kind to yourself" fluff, and anything that sounds like a self-help book.

Blog readers: friends, some former students, people from a quit-smoking forum.

How he answers: blunt, a teacher's habit of correcting framing.


# Person B

# Ruth (task: resolutions)
67, retired accountant, widowed, writes a blog mostly read by other retirees in her town.

What she thinks: resolutions fail because they're about improving yourself on your own, and nobody is waiting for you to show up. The ones that worked in her life were the ones where someone else was counting on her.

Real case: she resolved to walk more for years and never did. Then her neighbour Joan, recently out of hip surgery, needed someone to walk with, and they've walked every weekday at 9 for two years. "I'd never let Joan down. I let myself down all the time."

Also: she thinks a lot of resolution writing is aimed at young people trying to be productive, and retirees are trying to stay connected, not productive.

How she answers: longer, with small anecdotes; polite; sometimes goes off on a tangent.
