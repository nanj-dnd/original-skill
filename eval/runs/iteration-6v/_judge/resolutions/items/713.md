# Nothing Grows in January

Every January, a lot of people decide to change their lives on one of the coldest, darkest, least promising days of the year. By the time the snowdrops are up, plenty of them have given up. We call that a failure of willpower. I think it's a failure of timing.

Any gardener could have told them. Outside, nothing grows in January. The soil is cold and the days are short. Anything you put in the ground now will sit there and rot, or come up weak and get flattened by the next frost. Nobody calls that seed lazy. We just say it went in at the wrong time.

New Year's resolutions go in at the wrong time. Decide on January 1 to run every morning, and you're asking yourself to start in the dark and the cold, with the holidays still sitting heavy and nothing outside to pull you out the door. When it doesn't take, you blame yourself. But really you sowed tomatoes in a snowbank.

Gardeners don't make that mistake, because we already know what winter is for. Winter is for planning. It's for the kitchen table, the catalogs and a cup of tea. You sketch the beds and decide what gets moved, what gets dug out, and what you're finally going to try this year. In some ways it's the most hopeful season in the garden, because nothing is being asked of it yet. The doing comes later, when the ground is ready.

So if you want a resolution that lasts, treat it like a planting plan. Use January to decide, not to start. Write it down the way you'd write down what's going in the vegetable bed. Then give it a start date the way you'd give a seed one: after your last frost, when the light is back and the days are long enough to carry you. (If you garden somewhere that January is high summer, you already know your own dead month. Plan in that one instead.)

That isn't putting it off. It's what we already do with every seed we care about. Nobody thinks a gardener who waits for May to plant out the beans has given up on beans.

People will tell you most resolutions fail, and maybe they do. But most of them never got a fair chance to grow. Plan yours this winter. Plant it in spring.
