## Thesis

Social media has been good for democracy as participation and bad for it as governance. By making it dramatically easier for citizens to find one another and mobilize, it has expanded participatory democracy. But by bypassing linkage institutions and removing the friction Madison built into the extended republic, it has weakened the parties, organized groups and deliberation that turn public energy into durable policy and peaceful compromise.

## Outline (about 1,500 words)

**I. Introduction (about 175 words)**
- **Opening:** Social media is usually judged by what it lets people *do*, such as protest, post and organize. Democracy also has to *decide* and *govern*. Judging the platforms by mobilization alone captures only half the picture.
- **Definition of democracy:** Keep it broad, as you asked, and define it explicitly. One option from the AP Government curriculum is the three models:
  - Participatory democracy: broad citizen involvement.
  - Pluralist democracy: organized groups competing for influence.
  - Elite democracy: limited participation, with decisions filtered through representatives and institutions.
  - State that the essay judges social media against all three.
- **Thesis.**
- **Roadmap:** Three familiar cases, each reread to separate mobilization from governance.

**II. The participatory gain is real (about 225 words)**
- Social media lowers the cost of political action. Citizens can find like-minded people, coordinate quickly and reach a mass audience without money or institutional backing.
- By the participatory model's standard, this is a clear democratic good. More people can enter politics, and faster.
- **Federalist No. 10 link:** Madison argued that a large republic would make it harder for people with a shared grievance to "discover their own strength" and "act in unison." Social media removes that barrier almost entirely.
- **Pivot:** Whether that is good or bad depends on what happens *after* people mobilize. The three cases test that.

**III. Case 1: Egypt, 2011, reread (about 275 words)**
- **Standard reading:** The "Facebook revolution" shows social media empowering citizens against a dictator. Mubarak fell after eighteen days of protest.
- **Against the grain:** The networked youth movement that filled Tahrir Square did not win the elections that followed. The Muslim Brotherhood won the 2012 presidency. It had spent decades building offline organization, and the youth movement had not. The military removed President Morsi in 2013.
- **Mechanism:** Speed of mobilization stood in for organizational capacity. A movement that can gather a million people in days has not necessarily built the structures that negotiate, field candidates or govern.
- **Point for the thesis:** This is participatory success alongside a failure in what the pluralist model requires, which is organized, durable groups.

**IV. Case 2: The 2016 US election and Cambridge Analytica, reread (about 275 words)**
- **Standard reading:** Data-driven micro-targeting manipulated voters.
- **Against the grain:** The evidence that psychographic targeting changed votes is weak. The UK Information Commissioner's 2020 investigation found Cambridge Analytica's methods were largely standard and less powerful than the firm advertised. The deeper story is structural. Social media let candidates reach voters directly and build movements outside party control.
- **Mechanism:** Parties are *linkage institutions*, and one of their jobs is to screen candidates and build coalitions. Direct-to-voter platforms weaken that gatekeeping role.
- **Balance:** This cuts both ways. Weaker gatekeeping opens politics to outsiders, which is a participatory good. It also removes a filter that elite and pluralist models depend on.
- **Point for the thesis:** The harm is not mind control. It is that social media hollows out an institution that helps the system govern.

**V. Case 3: The 2020 racial justice protests, reread (about 250 words)**
- **Standard reading:** Social media spread video of George Floyd's killing and produced one of the largest protest movements in US history.
- **Against the grain:** The mobilization was enormous, but the federal legislative result was limited. The George Floyd Justice in Policing Act passed the House in 2021. Bipartisan Senate negotiations then collapsed, and no federal bill became law.
- **Mechanism:** Turning public pressure into a statute requires sustained interest-group work, coalition building and compromise in Congress. Those slow processes are the ones viral mobilization tends to skip.
- **Fairness:** Acknowledge real state and local policy changes, and note that the Senate's own rules mattered. This keeps the claim measured.

**VI. Counterargument and response (about 175 words)**
- **Counterargument:** Each failure has non-digital causes, including military power in Egypt, polarization in the US and the filibuster in the Senate. Social media is being blamed for problems it did not create.
- **Response:** Grant the point partly. Then narrow the claim: social media did not create these obstacles, but it shifted citizens' energy toward fast mobilization and away from building the organizations needed to overcome them.
- **Contrast:** The civil rights movement of the 1950s and 1960s relied on churches, the NAACP and years of slow organizing. It produced the Civil Rights Act of 1964.

**VII. Conclusion (about 125 words)**
- **Restate the thesis:** Social media is good for the first step of democracy and weak at the steps that follow.
- **Return to the definition:** Social media raises participatory democracy but weakens the pluralist and elite structures that translate participation into policy.
- **Closing line:** The question for American democracy is whether citizens can rebuild the organizations that social media lets them skip, not whether they can make their voices heard.
