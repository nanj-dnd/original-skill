## Thesis

Asking whether social media has been good or bad for democracy treats very different technologies as one. Its democratic effects follow from design: public, algorithmically ranked feeds and private, encrypted messaging apps shape participation, information and accountability in different ways. India's 2018 WhatsApp lynchings, and the forwarding limit that followed, show that changing how information travels can reach harms that rules about content cannot.

## Outline (about 1,500 words)

**I. Introduction (~200)**
- Open with India in mid-2018. Child-kidnapping rumours forwarded on WhatsApp were linked to a series of mob killings. In July, WhatsApp responded by limiting forwards in India to five chats and labelling forwarded messages. It changed the product and removed no posts.
- Make the point: the company could not police content it could not read, so it changed the plumbing.
- State the thesis, then preview the three mechanisms.

**II. Two designs, not one "social media" (~200)**
- *Public feeds* (Facebook News Feed, X, YouTube) are one-to-many, ranked by algorithm, and visible to outsiders.
- *Private messaging* (WhatsApp, Telegram groups, Signal) is one-to-few. It is unranked, end-to-end encrypted, and spreads through forwarding among trusted contacts.
- Explain why the distinction matters for a "good or bad" verdict. Most famous evidence (Cambridge Analytica, feed experiments) concerns public feeds. Much political communication in India, Brazil and Indonesia runs through messaging.

**III. Mechanism 1: Participation (~250)**
- *Feeds:* visibility lets movements mobilise fast. Tufekci (2017) argues this speed can come without the organisational capacity that slower movements built, which makes them easier to scatter after the peak.
- *Messaging:* the cost of joining is low wherever cheap smartphones dominate. Groups map onto family, neighbourhood, caste and party networks, so political talk moves through existing social ties rather than among strangers.
- Claim: each design widens participation, but for different groups and with different durability.

**IV. Mechanism 2: Information (~300)**
- *Feeds:* the usual worry is algorithmic ranking. Note the complication: Guess et al. (2023, *Science*) switched some US users to chronological feeds during the 2020 election. Time on the platform fell, but polarisation did not change significantly. Ranking may matter less than often assumed.
- *Messaging:* there is no algorithm. Trust in the sender replaces the credibility of the source, and forwarding strips away where a message came from. Banaji & Bhat (2019) found that pre-existing prejudice shaped which rumours people believed and passed on in the Indian cases.
- Claim: the same false rumour behaves differently in each design. On a feed it is a ranking problem; in a chat it is a trust problem.

**V. Mechanism 3: Accountability (~300)**
- *Feeds:* public content can be seen by journalists, researchers, fact-checkers and moderators, however imperfectly.
- *Messaging:* encryption hides content from everyone, including the platform. This protects dissidents and journalists, but it means content regulation has nothing to act on.
- India as the test case:
  - WhatsApp's 2018 forward limit and labels were design changes that left encryption intact.
  - The government's alternative was the 2021 IT Rules' "traceability" requirement to identify a message's first originator. WhatsApp challenged it in the Delhi High Court as incompatible with encryption.
- Claim: for private messaging, the democratic choice is between design friction and weakening privacy. That trade-off doesn't exist for feeds in the same form.

**VI. Objection and reply (~150)**
- Objection: the violence had social roots, and de Freitas Melo et al. (2019) found that forward limits delay viral spread but do not prevent it.
- Reply: design does not create prejudice. It sets how fast and how far a rumour travels before anyone can respond, which in a mob situation can be decisive. The thesis is about which levers exist, not a claim that design alone explains outcomes.

**VII. Conclusion (~100)**
- Return to the question: "good or bad" has no single answer because there is no single technology.
- The better questions for scholars and regulators are which design, which mechanism, and which lever.
