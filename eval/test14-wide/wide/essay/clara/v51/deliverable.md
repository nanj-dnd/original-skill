**Angle**: Social media has no fixed effect on democracy; its effect is set by the institutions that receive what the platforms produce and by the design of the feeds themselves, and the Arab Spring, run as a Tunisia–Egypt comparison, is the cleanest evidence for that.

# Thesis

Social media has been neither good nor bad for democracy in itself. Its effect is determined by two variables the question omits: the strength of the institutions that receive what platforms produce, including courts, parties, an independent press, and a military under civilian control, and the design of the platforms, above all whether a feed ranks content by time or by engagement. Where institutions are strong and feeds are plain, social media widens participation; where institutions are weak or feeds reward outrage, it amplifies whoever already stands to gain from disorder. The Arab Spring shows both outcomes from the same platforms in the same months.

# Outline (1,500 words)

## I. Introduction (175 words)
- Open with Tunisia and Egypt in January and February 2011. The same platforms, the same tactics (a Facebook page, a hashtag, a date for the square), and the same result inside weeks: Ben Ali fled on January 14, Mubarak resigned on February 11. Then the paths split. Tunisia had a new constitution by 2014 and its civil-society negotiators won the 2015 Nobel Peace Prize; Egypt had a military coup in July 2013 and has had a general in power since.
- The point of the pairing: when the tool is held constant and outcomes still diverge, the tool is not what decided them.
- Define the terms an AP Government reader expects: social media as a linkage institution, democracy as a system that needs participation, accountability, and losers who accept results.
- State the thesis and name the two variables.

## II. What social media does under favorable conditions (325 words)
- Lowers the cost of participation. Organizing once required money, a party, or a newspaper; in 2011 it required a Facebook page. Wael Ghonim's "We Are All Khaled Said" page named the date of the first Cairo protest. The Egyptian government's five-day internet shutdown beginning January 28 is evidence the regime believed the platforms mattered.
- Breaks gatekeeping. In the United States, phone video and hashtags carried stories legacy outlets had not prioritized (Black Lives Matter after 2014, #MeToo in 2017), and small-dollar online fundraising changed who can run for office.
- Connect to the course: Federalist 10 assumed factions would be slowed by distance and the difficulty of communicating across a large republic. Social media removes that friction. Madison's safeguard against faction was the extended sphere; the extended sphere now fits in a pocket. This is the benefit and, as Section III shows, the cost.
- Close the section honestly: all of these gains occurred where there was something to join or to appeal to: a court, an election, a press that could pick the story up.

## III. What social media does under unfavorable conditions (325 words)
- Myanmar, 2017. Facebook was, for most users, the internet; the company had almost no Burmese-language moderation; military-linked pages spread anti-Rohingya content. The UN fact-finding mission in 2018 concluded the platform had played a "determining role," and Facebook's own commissioned review that year acknowledged it had failed to prevent its use to incite violence. There was no independent press and no civilian control of the army to absorb the shock.
- Egypt after 2013. The platforms that mobilized Tahrir Square were used by the Sisi government for surveillance and prosecution of activists. The tool did not change; the institution holding it did.
- The United States, 2020 to 2021. The "stolen election" narrative spread through the same sharing mechanics, and January 6 was organized in public on the platforms.
- The design point: Vosoughi, Roy, and Aral (Science, 2018) found false news on Twitter spread farther and faster than true news. Harm here is not simply a matter of bad users; it is a matter of what the ranking rewards.
- Transition: these harms are real, but each one has a second cause attached, a missing check or a design choice. That is the essay's argument.

## IV. Synthesis: the two variables (450 words)
**A. Institutions (225 words)**
- Tunisia versus Egypt, run as a controlled comparison. Both had the platforms, the youth bulge, and the grievance. Tunisia's army stayed out of politics and its unions, employers, lawyers, and human-rights league (the National Dialogue Quartet) negotiated the transition. Egypt's military was the state's strongest institution and reclaimed power when the elected government faltered. Social media opened the door in both; what stood behind the door differed.
- January 6 as the American version of the test. The platforms carried the mobilization. Dozens of lawsuits failed in court, state officials of both parties certified results, and Congress completed the count the same night. The outcome was set by institutional strength, not by the feed.
- Supporting evidence: Boxell, Gentzkow, and Shapiro (2017) found polarization in the United States grew fastest among the oldest Americans, the group least likely to use social media, which suggests party sorting and cable news carry much of the weight the default verdict assigns to platforms.

**B. Design (225 words)**
- The 2011 platform was not the 2017 platform. Twitter's timeline was chronological until 2016. Facebook reweighted its News Feed in 2018 to favor reshares and comments, and the internal research released by Frances Haugen in 2021 showed the company's own staff found the change favored divisive content.
- Myanmar and the 2020 election therefore do not refute the Arab Spring; they describe a different product. A chronological feed is a bulletin board; an engagement-ranked feed is an editor with one criterion.
- Course connection: this is why the policy debate (Section 230, algorithmic transparency, moderation rules) is about design rather than about whether people may speak. The First Amendment protects the speech; it does not require a particular ranking.

## V. Counterargument and reply (125 words)
- Objection: weak institutions and engagement-ranked feeds are now the normal case, so in practice the verdict is "bad," and the conditional thesis is a technicality.
- Reply: the objection concedes the argument. If the harm depends on design and institutional strength, then it is a policy problem with a policy answer, not a property of the technology, and the answer differs by country. Myanmar's problem was the absence of any check; the American problem was the ranking. Treating both as "social media is bad" prescribes nothing.

## VI. Conclusion (100 words)
- The question assumes a fixed effect; the evidence shows a variable one. Return to the opening: Tunisia and Egypt used the same platforms in the same month and ended in different places because of what their institutions did next.
- The two variables that decide the outcome, institutional strength and feed design, are the two things a democracy can actually change. That is the reason the conditional answer is more useful than either verdict.
