**Angle**: The confident verdicts on both sides outrun the evidence; what the best studies actually support is a conditional claim — social media has mostly helped where democracy was weak and mostly hurt where it was already polarized — so the essay's job is to test "good or bad" against the research and show where each answer holds.
**Built from**: 1d (undecided, test the question) sets the stance; 4a (grader, measured, sourced) makes the evidence the spine; 3d sends it to the less-used studies and cases; 2d keeps it global.
**Strongest objection**: "A thesis that says 'it depends' is a dodge, and graders punish fence-sitting." **Test**: read the thesis aloud and ask whether someone could disagree with it. They can — a "net bad" partisan rejects the "mostly helped where weak" half, and a "net good" partisan rejects the "hurt where polarized" half. If your course rubric says "take a clear side," use the Other direction instead.
**Other direction**: Commit to "net bad, but for a different reason than usually given": not outrage algorithms, but the collapse of the local-news economy that platforms' ad capture caused — a mechanism the samples barely touch.

# Thesis

Public debate about social media and democracy is dominated by two confident stories — that it liberated citizens from gatekeepers, or that it poisoned them with outrage — and the research supports neither as a general verdict. Across the strongest available evidence, three findings hold: social media reliably lowers the cost of political participation and protest; experiments that remove or alter it in established democracies produce only small changes in polarization; and its most serious documented harms cluster in countries where courts, press and electoral institutions were already weak. The defensible conclusion is therefore conditional rather than global: social media has tended to strengthen democratic pressure where democracy was fragile and to intensify existing divisions where democracy was established, and "good or bad" is the wrong unit of analysis.

# Outline

**I. Opening: a result that fits neither story (≈175 words)**
- Begin not with Arab Spring versus January 6 but with an experiment: in the weeks before the 2018 US midterms, economists paid about 1,600 Facebook users to deactivate their accounts for a month (Allcott, Braghieri, Eichmeyer and Gentzkow, *American Economic Review*, 2020). Polarization on some measures fell modestly; so did political knowledge; subjective well-being rose. Nobody's story predicted all three.
- Pose the question the essay will test: if a month without the largest platform barely moves the needle on polarization, what is the evidence for the strong claims in either direction?
- State the thesis and the method: treat "good for democracy" as a set of testable sub-claims rather than a verdict to be asserted.

**II. Setting the test: what "good for democracy" would have to mean (≈175 words)**
- Define three measurable components that most theories of democracy share: participation (can citizens act?), deliberation (can they reason together?), and institutional integrity (do elections, courts and the press hold?).
- Explain why this matters: the "good" camp usually argues from participation, the "bad" camp from deliberation, and they rarely meet. The essay scores all three.
- Name the evidence standard: prefer causal studies (experiments, natural experiments) and systematic reviews over anecdotes, because this topic is unusually rich in vivid stories and unusually poor in agreed facts.

**III. Test one — participation: the evidence favours "good" (≈275 words)**
- Natural experiments show social media lowers the cost of collective action. Enikolopov, Makarin and Petrova (*Econometrica*, 2020) found that where the Russian network VK spread earlier, protest participation in 2011–12 was higher, including among people who did not personally oppose the regime.
- Lorenz-Spreen and colleagues' systematic review of nearly 500 studies (*Nature Human Behaviour*, 2023) finds the most consistent positive associations are with political participation and news consumption.
- Less-worn cases than Tahrir Square: Belarus 2020, where the Telegram channel Nexta coordinated protests after the state shut down the internet; Iran 2022, where the Mahsa Amini protests spread through platforms the state had to throttle; Taiwan's g0v and Cofacts volunteer fact-checking, where civic tech runs on the same platforms.
- Caveat the finding honestly: participation is not the same as democratic outcome. Belarus's regime survived; Iran's did. Social media lowers the cost of showing up, not the cost of winning.

**IV. Test two — deliberation and polarization: the evidence is weaker than the "bad" story claims (≈325 words)**
- The strongest recent evidence comes from the Meta–academic collaboration around the 2020 US election (Guess et al. and Nyhan et al., *Science* and *Nature*, 2023): switching users to chronological feeds or removing reshared content for three months changed what people saw but did not measurably change their polarization or political attitudes.
- Boxell, Gentzkow and Shapiro (*PNAS*, 2017) found US polarization rose fastest among the over-65s, the group least likely to be online; their later cross-country work (*Review of Economics and Statistics*, 2024) shows affective polarization rising in the US but falling in several countries with comparable platform use, such as Germany and Norway.
- Present the counter-evidence fairly, because the essay is a test, not a brief: Bail et al. (*PNAS*, 2018) found that paying Twitter users to follow opposing accounts made them *more* partisan; the Lorenz-Spreen review finds consistent correlations between digital media use and polarization and populist support, strongest in established democracies.
- The honest reading: social media appears to be an accelerant for divisions that have other causes, and the size of its independent effect is contested and probably modest. A grader should see the student holding two bodies of evidence at once rather than choosing one.

**V. Test three — institutional integrity: harms concentrate where institutions are weak (≈300 words)**
- Where documented harm is gravest, the common factor is not the platform but the absence of a functioning counterweight. India's WhatsApp-linked lynchings of 2018 spread through forwarded rumours in areas with little local reporting to contradict them; Sri Lanka's 2018 anti-Muslim violence prompted a government block of Facebook after platform moderation failed in Sinhala; the Philippines' 2016 election saw coordinated disinformation operate largely unchecked by a press already under pressure.
- Contrast with established democracies, where the same tactics met friction: Taiwan's rapid-response fact-checking during Chinese influence operations; the EU's Digital Services Act creating legal obligations platforms lacked elsewhere.
- Tie to the review evidence: Lorenz-Spreen et al. find digital media's relationship to democracy differs by regime type, with more benefit in emerging democracies and autocracies and more harm in established ones on trust and polarization — the mirror image of the institutional-weakness pattern on violence and manipulation. The essay should flag this tension openly: the two effects run in different directions and the student is not obliged to resolve it, only to show it.

**VI. Objection and response (≈125 words)**
- Objection: a conditional verdict is unfalsifiable hedging. Response: the thesis makes claims that can be wrong. If deactivation and feed experiments in established democracies had shown large polarization effects, the second finding would fail; if protest studies showed no participation effect, the first would. Each half has been tested and could have come out otherwise.
- Second objection: the strongest experiments are American and may not travel. Concede it, and note that this is precisely why the global verdict should be held loosely.

**VII. Conclusion (≈125 words)**
- Return to the deactivation study: a month without Facebook did not fix American democracy or break it, and that null result is itself a finding.
- Restate the conditional thesis in fresh terms: social media is a cost-lowering technology, and what it makes cheaper depends on what citizens and institutions around it already wanted to do.
- Close on the implication for the question as set: asking whether social media is good or bad for democracy in general is like asking whether roads are good or bad for a city. The useful questions are about which roads, where, and who maintains them — and the essay has shown that the evidence can answer those where it cannot answer the first.
