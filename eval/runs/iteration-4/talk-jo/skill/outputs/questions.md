**Fixed vs open.** Fixed: 5 minutes (roughly 650–750 spoken words), a company all-hands next Friday, the topic of AI coding assistants. Open: what you actually claim, the angle, which examples, how it's structured, and who in the room you're talking to.

## What everyone gets

Main claim: "AI coding assistants are a powerful tool, not a replacement. Use them for boilerplate, tests and exploration, keep humans in the loop, and review everything."

Choices it made:
1. **Thesis**: a balanced "yes, but": use them a lot, not for everything.
2. **Angle**: the productivity upside against the quality and security risk.
3. **Examples**: generic ones (writing boilerplate, explaining old code, a hallucinated API or a security hole), plus a "studies show X% faster" stat.
4. **Structure**: hook question, pros, cons, guidelines, a call to "use it wisely".
5. **Reader**: engineers, with everyone else in the room as bystanders.
6. **Leaves out**: anything from your own team, what this means for juniors and skill growth, cost, and who is accountable when AI-written code breaks.

## A few questions before I write it

Answer in one line, e.g. `1b 2a 3: <your words> 4c 5: <your words>`. Your own words beat a letter anywhere.

**1. Your position.** The default answer is "use it a lot, but not for everything; review everything." Where do you actually stand?
- a) That, the balanced middle
- b) Further: engineers *should* use it for nearly everything, and the review-everything caution is outdated or slowing us down
- c) The other way: we're over-using it, and some work (design, debugging, learning) should stay deliberately human
- d) Your own words

**2. A real case.** The default runs on generic examples and an industry stat. Is there something from *your* company or team behind your view, such as a time an assistant saved real time, caused an incident, got through review with a bug, or changed how someone onboarded? Even a rough number helps ("cut a migration from 3 weeks to 4 days", "two outages traced to AI code last quarter").
- a) Yes: describe it in a sentence or two
- b) Not one case, but a pattern I've noticed: describe it
- c) Nothing specific; keep it general

**3. Who you're really talking to.** The default speaks to engineers, with everyone else listening in. At an all-hands, who do you most need to move?
- a) Engineers, about how they work day to day
- b) Leadership and managers: what to expect, measure or fund
- c) The non-engineers in the room: what this changes for them (timelines, quality, jobs)
- d) Your own words

**4. What you want to happen afterwards.** The default ends on "use it wisely", which asks no one to do anything. What should change after your 5 minutes?
- a) A specific policy or norm adopted (say which)
- b) People try something, or try it differently, next week
- c) Open a debate or question the company hasn't faced yet
- d) Your own words

**5. Why it's you up there.** The default could be given by anyone. What's your role, and do you have a stake in the answer, e.g. you lead a team that went all-in, you review a lot of AI-written PRs, or you're the one who got asked to set the policy?
