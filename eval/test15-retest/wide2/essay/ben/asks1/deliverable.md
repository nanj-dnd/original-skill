## Thesis

**Social media has made democracy easier to join but harder to sustain. It has lowered the barriers to participation and mobilization. But it has weakened the shared facts, trust, and deliberation that democracies need in order to govern themselves. On balance, it has been bad for democracy, though because of how platforms are designed, not because the technology itself is inherently harmful.**

---

## Outline (~1,500 words)

### I. Introduction (~200 words)
- **Hook:** Two contrasting images. Protesters in Cairo's Tahrir Square organizing on Facebook in 2011, and rioters coordinating online before the U.S. Capitol attack on January 6, 2021. Social media made both events possible.
- **Context:** In the 2000s, many people expected social media to democratize information and empower citizens. Today, many blame it for polarization and misinformation.
- **Define democracy for the essay:** It is more than voting. It needs (1) broad participation and (2) a functioning public sphere where citizens share basic facts and can disagree productively.
- **Thesis** (as above).
- **Roadmap:** Benefits to participation, then harms to the public sphere, then the counterargument, then why design is the root problem.

### II. The case for social media: participation and voice (~300 words)
- **Topic sentence:** Social media's strongest democratic contribution is that it lets ordinary people organize and be heard without gatekeepers.
- **Evidence:**
  - Arab Spring (2010–2011): activists used Facebook and Twitter to coordinate protests and broadcast them globally.
  - Movements like #MeToo and Black Lives Matter brought issues onto the national agenda that traditional media had underplayed.
  - Bond et al. (2012, *Nature*): a Facebook "I Voted" message shown to 61 million users measurably increased turnout.
- **Analysis:** This is real democratic value. It expands who gets a voice, especially marginalized groups.
- **Transition:** Participation is only half of democracy. What happens after people show up matters too.

### III. The case against: damage to the shared public sphere (~400 words)
This is the longest section because it carries the thesis.

- **Topic sentence:** The same features that make mobilization easy (speed, virality, and algorithmic amplification) undermine the shared reality democracy depends on.
- **Point A: Misinformation spreads faster than truth.**
  - Vosoughi, Roy & Aral (2018, *Science*): false news on Twitter spread farther and faster than true news, largely because people shared it.
  - Example: false claims about the 2020 U.S. election spread widely online and contributed to January 6.
- **Point B: Outrage is rewarded, which deepens polarization.**
  - Engagement-based feeds favor emotional, divisive content.
  - Bail et al. (2018, *PNAS*): exposing people to opposing views on Twitter sometimes made them *more* entrenched. This complicates the simple "filter bubble" story.
- **Point C: Manipulation and the erosion of trust.**
  - Cambridge Analytica harvested users' data for political targeting.
  - Myanmar: a 2018 UN fact-finding mission said Facebook played a role in spreading hate against the Rohingya.
- **Analysis:** These harms hit deliberation and trust, the parts of democracy that turn participation into self-government.

### IV. Counterargument and rebuttal (~300 words)
- **Counterargument:** Social media is being blamed for problems that have other causes.
  - Boxell, Gentzkow & Shapiro (2017): U.S. polarization grew fastest among older Americans, the group least likely to use social media.
  - Cable news, partisan talk radio, and long-term political sorting predate social media.
  - Large 2020 election studies with Meta (published in 2023 in *Science* and *Nature*) found that changing users' feeds for a few months didn't significantly change their polarization.
- **Rebuttal:**
  - Concede that social media isn't the *sole* cause.
  - But it acts as an accelerant. It amplifies existing divisions and gives bad actors cheap, global reach.
  - Short-term experiments may miss long-term, society-wide effects, such as how politicians and journalists change their behavior to chase virality.
  - Its harms don't need to be the only cause to outweigh its benefits.

### V. Why the problem is design, not the technology (~150 words)
- **Topic sentence:** The harms come from business models built on engagement and advertising, not from social media itself.
- Platforms profit from attention, and outrage captures attention.
- **Implication:** Different choices could keep the participation benefits while reducing the harms. Examples include chronological feeds, friction before resharing, transparency about algorithms, and regulation like the EU's Digital Services Act.
- This keeps your conclusion from being purely pessimistic.

### VI. Conclusion (~150 words)
- Return to the Tahrir Square / January 6 contrast. The tool was the same, but the democratic outcomes were opposite.
- Restate the thesis in fresh words: social media opened democracy's doors but weakened its foundations.
- **Closing thought:** The question isn't whether to have social media but who designs it and for what purpose. That is itself a democratic decision.
