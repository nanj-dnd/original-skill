# Resolutions Don't Fail. Attempts Do.

Every January someone posts the number: eighty percent of New Year's resolutions fail, most of them by February. Try tracing that figure to an actual study and it tends to dissolve into other articles quoting it. But where the number comes from matters less than what it counts.

In that math, a resolution fails the moment you break it. Smoke one cigarette in week five and you go in the failure column. Nobody checks back three years later to see whether you got there. The statistic measures attempts and reports the result as if it measured outcomes. Those are different things. Treat them as the same and a normal process starts to look like a verdict on your character.

I smoked for fifteen years. I've been clean for six. It took four serious tries.

By the January math, I failed three times. Here's what those three "failures" actually were. They're how I found my triggers. The first coffee of the day. Driving. My brother. Attempt two lasted five weeks and ended at a wedding. That's how I learned that weddings plus drinking were the thing that would get me.

So attempt four was planned around exactly that. What changed wasn't willpower. I didn't wake up one day a stronger person than the one who lit up at that reception. I just knew what would get me, and I knew it because I'd been got.

That's not unusual. People who study smoking cessation keep finding that most ex-smokers needed several serious attempts before one stuck. Quitting on the first try is the exception. If several attempts is what success normally looks like, then calling attempts one through three "failures" is just bad bookkeeping.

The usual answer to the eighty-percent problem is a list of tips: make it specific, make it small, track it in an app. That treats a broken resolution as a defect to engineer out. I think that's backwards. A broken attempt is the cheapest information you'll ever get about yourself. It tells you where, when and with whom the plan falls apart. You can't get that from a tip. You get it by trying and seeing what breaks.

Some of you reading this are on the forum, a few weeks or months past a relapse. I won't tell you how to feel about it. I'll tell you how I'd count it. You didn't fail a resolution. You ran an attempt, and it broke somewhere specific. Write down where.

Count attempts, not outcomes. Mine took four.

# Person B

# Ruth (task: resolutions)
67, retired accountant, widowed, writes a blog mostly read by other retirees in her town.

What she thinks: resolutions fail because they're about improving yourself on your own, and nobody is waiting for you to show up. The ones that worked in her life were the ones where someone else was counting on her.

Real case: she resolved to walk more for years and never did. Then her neighbour Joan, recently out of hip surgery, needed someone to walk with, and they've walked every weekday at 9 for two years. "I'd never let Joan down. I let myself down all the time."

Also: she thinks a lot of resolution writing is aimed at young people trying to be productive, and retirees are trying to stay connected, not productive.

How she answers: longer, with small anecdotes; polite; sometimes goes off on a tangent.
