## Shortlist

**A. The hidden 9-to-5** (1 + 3)
Resolution advice quietly assumes your day starts at the same time every day. When it doesn't, the method fails and you blame yourself. The fix is to tie the habit to an event ("after any shift ends") instead of a clock time.
- **Only yours because:** answer 1 (the method was never built for your life) and answer 2 (event, not clock).
- **Strongest objection:** "Habit stacking" and cue-based habits are already standard advice, so the tip alone isn't new. The new part is who it's aimed at.
- **Test:** shift-working readers recognize "same time every day" as advice that has already failed them.

**B. Your failures have a date on them** (2, carrying 3)
Your 6am gym habit died in February three years running, and every time it was when the rotation flipped you to nights. The pattern says it was the schedule, not you. Find where your habits break, then build the habit around that break instead of hoping to push through it. Ends on "first thing after any shift, day or night," 14 months and counting.
- **Only yours because:** your three-year case and the 14-month habit (answer 3). Nobody else can write this one.
- **Strongest objection:** one person's story could read as anecdote. A reader on a different rotation might not see their own pattern in it.
- **Test:** readers can check it against their own history: did their last broken habit die at a schedule change?

**C. Count per stretch, not per day** (7 + 14)
Streaks and "never miss a day" are built for weeks that repeat. On three nights then two days, set the target per block ("gym after two of my three nights") and plan the misses into it, so a hard stretch doesn't count as quitting.
- **Only yours because:** answer 1 (schedules that won't hold still) and answer 5 (three nights and then two days).
- **Strongest objection:** looser targets can let a habit slip quietly. Daily rules exist because they're hard to fudge.
- **Test:** it has to survive "per stretch is just a loophole." It needs a clear rule for what counts as a missed block.

**D. Don't start on January 1** *(unlikely)* (12)
January 1 is a 9-to-5 date. Half your readers are working it. Start the resolution on a rotation boundary instead (your first day off, or the first shift of a new block), so the habit is born inside your real schedule rather than a calendar that ignores it.
- **Only yours because:** answer 4 (nurses and shift workers, many working the holiday) and your rotation case.
- **Strongest objection:** it's a narrower idea, and could read as a gimmick if it's the whole post.
- **Test:** depends on holiday shifts being common for your readers, which is true in hospitals but worth stating carefully rather than overclaiming.

**E. Discipline is borrowed from the calendar** (4)
The people who make morning routines look easy mostly have a calendar that holds still, and it is doing half the work. If yours doesn't, you're not less disciplined. You're doing it without the help.
- **Only yours because:** answer 1 and answer 4 (readers who've heard the generic advice and felt worse).
- **Strongest objection:** it risks sounding like an excuse, or like it's knocking day workers. It needs the practical tips to keep it from becoming a complaint.
- **Test:** must survive a reader on rotating shifts who did keep a clock-based habit.

**My recommendation: B.** It's the only option built entirely on your own case. Three Februaries, then 14 months, makes the "not willpower" argument without having to argue it, and it carries the event-anchor tip naturally as the payoff. It also suits warm and a little wry. A's point can go inside it in one line.
