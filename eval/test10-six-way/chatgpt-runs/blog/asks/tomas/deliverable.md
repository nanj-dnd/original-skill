# Maybe You Haven't Failed Yet

Every January, millions of people make resolutions. By February, a good number have abandoned them. We call these failed resolutions, talk about a lack of discipline, blame unrealistic expectations, and move on to the next year.

I think we're looking at it all wrong.

We have an odd habit of judging an attempt by its outcome. If you set out to change something and don't manage it, you've failed. But why? Since when did the first attempt have to be the successful one?

I quit smoking on my fourth serious attempt. Six years later, I haven't touched a cigarette.

My first three attempts weren't particularly successful if you measure success by the number of days I stayed away from cigarettes. But each one taught me something.

The second attempt lasted five weeks. I eventually gave in at a wedding. That taught me something important: weddings, drinking and the social environment around them were triggers I hadn't accounted for.

Other attempts revealed different patterns. The first coffee of the morning. Driving. Even spending time with my brother.

By the fourth attempt, I wasn't simply telling myself I was going to quit. I knew what I was up against. I had identified the situations that made it difficult and planned around them.

Six years on, I find it difficult to call those first three attempts failures. Without them, I might never have made the fourth one work.

And this isn't particularly unusual. Research on smoking cessation has consistently found that many successful quitters make several attempts before stopping for good. The journey isn't always a straight line from decision to achievement.

Yet every January, we approach resolutions as though they come with a pass-or-fail deadline.

Perhaps we should stop doing that.

If you decide to exercise regularly and give up after three weeks, that's an attempt. If you try to save money and find yourself back where you started by March, that's an attempt too. The useful question isn't simply why you failed. It's what you discovered that you didn't know before.

What got in the way? What situation caught you off guard? What would you do differently next time?

I'm not suggesting that trying is the same as achieving. It isn't. But treating every unsuccessful attempt as a failure is a pretty good way of ensuring you never learn from it.

So, if your New Year's resolution didn't survive the year, don't just cross it off the list.

Look at what happened. There might be something in there worth taking into your next attempt.

You may not be starting again. You may simply be starting with better information.
